Bone Grafting (Hip)

Using a small piece of bone from your hip to fill a gap or strengthen an area of your jaw

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has this been recommended?

A bone graft uses a piece of your own bone to fill a gap, or to add extra bone where more strength or volume is needed — for example, to fill a cavity left after a cyst has been removed, to support a cleft site, or to reinforce an area of the jaw before further treatment such as dental implants. The hip (specifically the iliac crest, the bony ridge at the top of the pelvis) is a common and reliable source of this kind of bone, since a useful amount can be taken with minimal effect on the hip itself.

This is different from a free flap such as the DCIA (hip) free flap used for larger jaw reconstructions: a hip bone graft of this kind does not need its own blood vessels moved and rejoined using loupe magnification. Instead, the graft relies on the surrounding tissue at its new site to grow a blood supply into it over time, which makes it a smaller, more straightforward procedure suited to smaller defects.

2. What does the anaesthetic involve?

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable.

3. What does the procedure involve?

  • An incision is made through the skin directly over the bony ridge at the top of the hip (the iliac crest) — the length of this incision depends on how much bone is needed, and can be quite short if only a small amount is required.
  • A measured piece of bone is carefully removed from the iliac crest.
  • The hip wound is closed in layers with dissolvable stitches; a small drain is sometimes placed if a larger amount of bone has been taken, and is usually removed after a day or so.
  • The bone graft is then shaped and placed at the site needing it in your jaw, and secured as needed for your specific procedure.
  • The hole left in the iliac crest is not filled with anything artificial — it is left to heal on its own, and is as strong as before within a few months.

4. What to expect afterwards

Immediately afterwards

  • You should expect to stay in hospital for a couple of days, though your total length of stay depends on any other procedures carried out at the same time.
  • Your hip will be sore for at least a few days, even if only a small amount of bone was taken; if a larger amount was removed, soreness can last a couple of weeks.
  • Regular painkillers are provided, and, if a drain was placed, it is removed once drainage has settled, usually after a day or so.
  • Because your hip is sore, you may have some difficulty walking at first, and rarely a walking stick is needed for a few weeks afterwards.

As you recover

  • Soreness and difficulty walking gradually improve over the following days and weeks.
  • You should keep the wound dry until the dressing has settled; remember you cannot drive or operate machinery for 48 hours after a general anaesthetic, and longer if walking is still difficult, since you need to be able to perform an emergency stop safely.
  • The hip continues to heal fully over several months, eventually returning to its original strength.
  • Your progress with the grafted bone in your jaw is followed up regularly by your surgical team.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications are possible, but the great majority of patients recover well from this operation. The approximate figures below are drawn from Mr Maciver's own surgical complications library:

  • Infection at the graft site — occurs in around 1 to 5% of cases, and is managed with antibiotics and appropriate wound care.
  • Swelling around the graft site — common, but usually settles within a week or two.
  • Pain and discomfort — common after this type of procedure and generally resolves within a few days to weeks.
  • Delayed healing, which varies depending on your overall health and the amount of bone taken.
  • Graft failure, where the graft does not integrate properly with the existing bone — the risk varies with the type of graft and individual factors, estimated at around 1 to 10%.
  • Some degree of graft resorption (gradual reabsorption of the grafted bone) is expected over time, and the extent varies between patients.
  • Altered sensation or numbness near the graft site, due to nerve irritation — the risk is generally low.
  • Further surgery may occasionally be needed to manage a complication or if the graft has not taken as well as hoped.

Questions patients often ask

Is this the same as the DCIA hip free flap?

No — they use the same donor area (the hip) but are different procedures. This bone graft is a smaller amount of bone that relies on the surrounding tissue to grow a new blood supply into it. The DCIA free flap is a larger reconstruction where the bone is moved together with its own blood vessels, which are then rejoined using loupe magnification. Your surgeon will explain which is being used for you.

How much bone will be taken from my hip?

This depends entirely on what your jaw needs. Some procedures need only a small amount, taken through a short incision; others need more. Your surgeon will explain how much bone is planned for your specific case.

Will I be able to walk normally afterwards?

Most patients have some soreness and difficulty walking for the first few days to weeks, which gradually improves. A walking stick is rarely needed, and only for a short period.

Will I have a scar on my hip?

Yes, a scar remains at the donor site, though it is usually well hidden by clothing and fades over time. Its length depends on how much bone was taken.

How long will I be in hospital?

This depends on your hip graft and any other procedures carried out at the same time, but you should generally expect a couple of days in hospital.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, or bleeding at either the hip or jaw site. Use the contact details below.
