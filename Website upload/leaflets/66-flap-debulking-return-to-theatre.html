Flap Debulking and Return to Theatre

Further surgery to refine a healed flap's shape, or to check and support a flap in the days after your operation

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has this been recommended?

This leaflet covers two different situations that can follow flap reconstruction, and your surgeon will explain which one applies to you.

Flap debulking

A flap used to rebuild an area after surgery is often deliberately made a little larger and thicker than the final defect needs, since some shrinkage is expected as it settles and because it is safer to use slightly more tissue than to risk not having enough. Once healing is well established — usually several months after your original operation — some flaps are left thicker or bulkier than ideal for the best appearance or function, and a planned, elective operation to trim and refine the flap's shape (debulking) can improve this.

Return to theatre (flap take-back)

In the days immediately after free flap surgery, the flap's blood supply is monitored very closely, usually with checks every hour at first. If these checks suggest the blood supply may be compromised — for example if the artery or vein feeding the flap develops a blood clot — an urgent return to the operating theatre (a 'take-back') is needed to explore the blood vessels and try to restore normal blood flow, giving the flap the best chance of survival. This is an emergency rather than a planned procedure, and your team will explain the situation to you and your family as it develops.

2. What does the anaesthetic involve?

Both flap debulking and a return to theatre are carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Where time allows (as for a planned debulking procedure), you will have an anaesthetic assessment beforehand to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Where an urgent return to theatre is needed, this assessment is done as quickly as safely possible given the urgency of restoring the flap's blood supply. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable.

3. What does the procedure involve?

Flap debulking

  • The existing flap scar is reopened, and excess fat and soft tissue beneath the skin is carefully trimmed away to reduce the flap's bulk.
  • Great care is taken to protect the flap's blood supply throughout, since this is what keeps the tissue alive.
  • The skin is then re-draped and closed with dissolvable stitches, aiming for a more refined, natural contour.
  • This is sometimes done in more than one stage for a larger area, allowing the tissue to settle between procedures.

Return to theatre (take-back)

  • The original wound is reopened and the flap's artery and vein are carefully examined.
  • If a blood clot is found, it is removed and the vessel is repaired or rejoined as needed to restore blood flow.
  • The flap's blood supply is then reassessed to confirm it has been successfully restored.
  • If the flap's blood supply cannot be restored despite this, the flap may not survive, and your surgeon will discuss an alternative method of reconstruction with you and your family.

4. What to expect afterwards

Immediately afterwards

  • After a planned debulking procedure, this is often a day-case or overnight-stay procedure, and most patients recover quickly.
  • After an urgent return to theatre, you will usually spend a further period in a high-dependency or intensive care unit for close monitoring of the flap's blood supply, similar to after your original operation.
  • Some swelling, bruising, and discomfort at the site are normal after either procedure, and regular pain relief is provided.

As you recover

  • Swelling and bruising settle over the following weeks, and any stitches dissolve on their own.
  • After debulking, the area continues to soften and settle into its final shape over several months.
  • After a successful take-back, the flap is watched closely for a further period to confirm it continues to do well, in the same way as after your original surgery.
  • Your progress is followed up regularly with your surgical team.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications are possible, but the great majority of patients recover well from this operation, and your team monitors closely for these and has clear plans to manage them:

  • Infection — the risk varies with your overall health and the surgery involved, and is generally low to moderate; managed with antibiotics if it occurs.
  • A collection of blood (haematoma) or fluid (seroma) at the surgical site, which may need drainage.
  • The wound edge partially opening (wound dehiscence), which can delay healing.
  • Flap compromise — altering the flap's size or re-exploring its blood vessels can itself put the flap's blood supply at risk, potentially leading to partial or complete flap failure; your surgical team takes great care to minimise this.
  • Reduced tissue survival (necrosis) if blood flow to part of the flap is affected.
  • Delayed healing, related to the complexity of the surgery and the amount of tissue handled.
  • Scarring at the surgical site, usually manageable and settling over time.
  • A change in sensation in the flap, which can vary and may or may not settle.
  • Some asymmetry, or a change in the flap's contour, which can be difficult to make perfectly symmetrical or predict in advance.
  • Persistent swelling, which usually settles over time but can occasionally be prolonged.
  • Depending on the flap's location, some impact on function such as movement or speech, which varies with the individual case.
  • General risks of anaesthesia, including allergic reaction, and, for patients with pre-existing heart or lung conditions, a low risk of cardiac or pulmonary complications related to the stress of the procedure.

Questions patients often ask

Is flap debulking the same as a return to theatre?

No — they are different situations. Debulking is a planned, elective procedure done once your flap has fully healed, to refine its shape. A return to theatre (take-back) is an emergency procedure done in the days after your original operation if the flap's blood supply appears to be at risk.

Why does my flap need debulking?

Flaps are often made a little larger than strictly necessary to allow for shrinkage and to make sure there is enough tissue to reliably cover the defect. Once healing is complete, some flaps are left thicker than ideal, and a planned procedure to trim and refine the shape can improve the final appearance and, where relevant, function.

Why has my flap needed to go back to theatre?

This happens when monitoring suggests the flap's blood supply may be compromised, most often from a blood clot in the artery or vein feeding it. Returning to theatre promptly gives the best chance of restoring blood flow and saving the flap. Your surgical team will explain your specific situation to you and your family.

What happens if the flap can't be saved?

If the blood supply cannot be restored despite a return to theatre, the flap may not survive. If this happens, your surgeon will discuss with you and your family what alternative method of reconstruction is most suitable.

How many debulking procedures will I need?

This varies between patients. Some flaps need no further surgery at all; others benefit from one or occasionally more debulking procedures, sometimes staged to allow the tissue to settle in between. Your surgeon will advise you based on how your flap is settling.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, or any change in the colour or warmth of the flap. Use the contact details below.
