Obstructive Sleep Apnoea and Jaw Surgery (Maxillomandibular Advancement)

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains obstructive sleep apnoea and when moving the jaws forward (maxillomandibular advancement) may be considered. Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team will talk it through with you.

At a glance
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is when the airway at the back of the throat repeatedly closes during sleep.
  • Treatment starts with sleep-medicine assessment, lifestyle changes and usually a CPAP breathing machine or a mouth device.
  • Surgery is only considered after a sleep study and sleep-medicine review, usually when other treatments have not worked or cannot be used.
  • Maxillomandibular advancement (MMA) moves the upper and lower jaws forward to widen the airway. It is a major operation under general anaesthetic.
  • Most people improve a lot but not everyone is cured, so a follow-up sleep study is done.

1. Why has this operation been recommended?

In obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) the muscles of the throat relax in sleep and the airway narrows or closes. Breathing pauses, oxygen levels dip and the brain briefly wakes you to restart breathing, often without you knowing. Symptoms include loud snoring, gasping or choking in the night, unrefreshed sleep, morning headaches and tiredness in the day. Untreated, OSA raises the risk of high blood pressure, heart problems, stroke and accidents from sleepiness.

OSA is diagnosed by a sleep study and is managed by a sleep medicine or respiratory team. First-line treatments are weight loss if needed, avoiding alcohol and sedatives in the evening, sleeping on your side, and for moderate to severe OSA a CPAP machine that gently blows air to keep the airway open. A custom-made mouth device (mandibular advancement splint) helps some people.

Jaw surgery follows this assessment. It is considered if CPAP and other treatments have not worked or cannot be tolerated, and if your scans and examination show that the jaws are set back and the airway is narrow behind the tongue and soft palate. Your surgeon will review your sleep study and discuss with the sleep team whether it is right for you. Other operations to the nose, tongue or soft palate, or a nerve stimulator, may be options for some people.

2. The anaesthetic

Maxillomandibular advancement is done under general anaesthetic, so you will be fully asleep. Because OSA can make anaesthetics more complicated, your anaesthetist will plan this carefully with you. You must not drive or operate machinery for 48 hours afterwards.

Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

3. What does the procedure involve?

Maxillomandibular advancement (MMA) moves both jaws forward together. The tongue and soft palate are attached to the jaws, so they are pulled forward and the airway behind them widens. It is a type of orthognathic (corrective jaw) operation, described in leaflets 85 to 87.

  • Braces are often placed beforehand so that the teeth meet well after the jaws are moved. Planning uses scans and 3D models.
  • Cuts are made inside the mouth. The upper jaw (Le Fort I) and lower jaw (BSSO) are cut, moved forward by the planned amount and held in position with small titanium plates and screws.
  • The cuts inside the mouth are closed with dissolvable stitches. There is normally no scar on your face.
  • The operation takes several hours.

4. What to expect afterwards

  • Hospital stay. Usually one to three nights. Sometimes a night in a higher-care bed is advised because of the sleep apnoea. You may use CPAP for a few nights.
  • Swelling and bruising. Marked swelling of the face for a couple of weeks. Numbness of the lower lip, chin and upper lip is common at first.
  • Eating. A liquid then soft diet for several weeks while the bones heal. Mouth care with a soft brush and mouthwash is important.
  • Time off. Around 2 to 4 weeks off work, more for heavy work.
  • Follow-up. Check-ups with your surgeon and, after about 3 to 6 months, a repeat sleep study to see how well it has worked.
  • Results. In studies, most people (around 8 in 10) have a big improvement, and a smaller group are completely cured. A few see little change. Some need to continue CPAP or have further treatment.

5. Possible complications

Your surgeon will discuss these with you in detail. They are the same as for other double-jaw surgery (leaflets 85 to 87).

  • Swelling, bruising, pain and some bleeding (common).
  • Numbness of the lower lip and chin, and sometimes the upper lip and cheeks. This is usual early on and usually improves over months. A small number of people have lasting numbness.
  • Infection, which is uncommon. Antibiotics are given.
  • A change in your bite, or the need for adjustments with braces.
  • Change in the look of your face, which may be a noticeable forward position of the jaws and mouth. This is discussed and planned with you beforehand.
  • Problems with the bone plates, a bone that does not heal well, or relapse (the jaw drifting back slightly), all uncommon.
  • The operation not fully curing the sleep apnoea. Surgery is not suitable for everyone, and no operation works for every person.

Questions patients often ask

Can I go straight to surgery without trying CPAP?

Usually not. Sleep-medicine assessment, a sleep study and trial of other treatments come first, unless you have a clear jaw problem.

Will it change how I look?

Yes, it can. Moving the jaws forward changes the profile. Your surgeon will show you the planned change beforehand.

Will I be able to stop CPAP?

Many people can, but this is only decided after a repeat sleep study.

Is it only for people who are overweight?

No. OSA can affect people of any size, and jaw shape plays a part in some.
