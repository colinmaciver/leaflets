Nasal and Paranasal Sinus Cancer

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains cancer of the nose and the air spaces around it (the sinuses). It is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team. Your own plan will be agreed with you in person.

At a glance
  • These cancers are uncommon. Most start in the cheek sinus (maxillary sinus).
  • Symptoms often look like a sinus infection: a blocked nose, nosebleeds on one side, loss of smell, facial numbness.
  • Diagnosis uses a camera test, a biopsy and CT and MRI scans.
  • Treatment is surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy or a combination, planned by a specialist team.
  • Surgery is planned with care to protect the eye, the face and your appearance.

1. What is nasal and paranasal sinus cancer and why does it happen?

The nasal cavity is the air-filled space behind the nose. It warms and moistens the air you breathe and gives you your sense of smell. The paranasal sinuses are air-filled spaces around it: in the cheeks (maxillary sinuses), above the eyes (frontal sinuses), between the eyes (ethmoid sinuses) and further back (sphenoid sinuses). They give your voice its tone and lighten the skull.

Cancer happens when cells become abnormal, grow in an uncontrolled way and can spread. Different types can start here. The most common are squamous cell carcinoma, from the lining, and adenocarcinoma, from gland cells. Most start in the maxillary sinus in the cheek. These cancers are rare.

What causes it?

  • Breathing in dust or chemicals at work: wood dust, leather dust, nickel, chromium, formaldehyde, textile fibres and mineral oils.
  • Smoking or chewing tobacco.
  • Heavy alcohol use, especially with smoking.
  • Other factors: being male (about twice as common in men) and age (most are 45 or over). In many people no cause is found.

Possible symptoms

  • A blocked nose that does not clear, or reduced or lost sense of smell.
  • Nosebleeds, especially on one side.
  • Pressure or pain behind the nose or around the upper teeth, and loose or painful teeth or dentures.
  • A lump or sore inside the nose or mouth or on the face.
  • Facial numbness or tingling, headaches or sinus pain.
  • Eye problems such as a watery or bulging eye, double vision or loss of vision.
  • Ear pain or pressure.

Most people with these symptoms have a common sinus problem. But if they last more than a few weeks, especially on one side, please have them checked.

2. How is it diagnosed?

Your surgeon will ask about your symptoms, work and smoking history, and examine your nose, mouth, face, eyes and neck. Not everyone needs every test.

  • Nasoendoscopy: a thin flexible camera passed through the nose, done in clinic.
  • Biopsy: a small sample taken through the nose with a telescope, under local or general anaesthetic. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. There may be some bleeding afterwards, and you may need to stop blood thinners first.
  • Needle biopsy (FNA) of a neck lump, guided by ultrasound.
  • CT and MRI scans show the size of the cancer and how close it is to the eye, brain and skull base. PET scans look for spread elsewhere.

Staging

Staging describes the size of the cancer and whether it has spread (T, N and M). Stages I and II are small and early. Stages III and IV are more advanced. Many people with more advanced stages are still treated with the aim of cure.

3. Your specialist team

Your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

The team includes head and neck surgeons, reconstructive surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, oncologists (cancer doctors who give radiotherapy and chemotherapy), specialist nurses, speech and swallowing therapists, dietitians, dental and oral medicine specialists, and psychological and social support. You are always welcome to ask for a second opinion.

4. Treatment options

The best treatment depends on the type, size and position of the cancer, whether it has spread, your general health and your wishes. Treatment often involves surgery and radiotherapy together. Surgeons from other specialties, such as brain and eye surgeons, may join the team if the cancer is close to the skull base or the eye.

Surgery

Surgery is done under general anaesthetic. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

  • Endoscopic surgery: a telescope and instruments passed through the nose remove the cancer without cuts on the face. It suits some smaller cancers and can reach as far as the skull base.
  • Open surgery with removal of part of the upper jaw or cheek bone (maxillectomy): used for larger cancers. A plate or a dental prosthesis (an obturator) or tissue from another part of the body is used to rebuild the area so you can eat, speak and look as normal as possible.
  • Craniofacial resection: for cancers growing into or close to the skull. It is done through the face and the skull together with a brain surgeon.
  • Orbital exenteration: removal of the eye and the tissues in the eye socket, only if the cancer has grown into the socket and cannot be treated safely any other way. If this is ever being considered, it will be discussed with you in detail, and rehabilitation and an artificial prosthesis are options.
  • Neck dissection: removal of lymph nodes from the neck if the cancer has spread to them or is at risk of doing so.
  • Free flap reconstruction: if a larger area of tissue is removed, skin and muscle (and sometimes bone) with their blood supply are taken from another part of the body and used to rebuild the area. In published studies about 5 in 100 flaps (around 5%; most reports give between 3 and 6 in 100) do not survive and need further surgery.
  • Temporary tracheostomy: a breathing tube placed in the windpipe through a small opening in the lower neck, used only after bigger operations while swelling settles. It is usually removed within about a week once normal breathing is safe.
  • Feeding tubes: a tube through the nose for days or weeks, or through the skin into the stomach if feeding support is needed for longer.

A small drain is occasionally used and stitches are dissolvable. Your surgeon will tell you how long you will stay in hospital, which depends on the operation. Risks depend on the operation chosen, and your surgeon will explain those that apply to you, including bleeding, infection, changes to sense of smell, eye problems, fluid leak around the brain (uncommon) and changes in appearance or speech.

Radiotherapy and chemotherapy

  • Radiotherapy alone or with chemotherapy as the main treatment: daily on weekdays for about 7 weeks. It is used for some cancers, or when surgery is not suitable.
  • After surgery: radiotherapy, sometimes with chemotherapy, usually starts about 4 weeks after surgery and lasts about 6 weeks. It is given if the pathology shows a risk of the cancer returning. Not everyone needs it.
  • Palliative radiotherapy may be used to relieve pain, bleeding or pressure if a cure is not possible.

Side effects can include:

  • tiredness
  • sore, red or dry skin in the treated area
  • a dry or sore nose and mouth, crusting in the nose, and changes in taste and smell
  • watery or dry eyes, and eye problems in the treated area (the eyes are protected as far as possible)
  • tiredness and weight loss

Most side effects start around two weeks into treatment, are at their worst just after it finishes and improve over the following 4 to 6 weeks. Some can last for months and a few may be permanent, such as a dry mouth. Because radiotherapy can affect the jaw bone, any broken or infected teeth are usually treated before it starts, and regular dental check-ups afterwards are important.

5. Follow-up and outlook

After treatment you will have regular check-ups of your face, mouth, nose, throat and neck, with endoscopic checks and sometimes CT, MRI or PET scans. Keeping to these means any return is found early.

The outlook depends on the type, size and position of the cancer, whether it has spread, and how completely it can be treated. Your team will talk through what this means for you in a personal and honest conversation. Support is available for eating, speech, appearance and mood.

If you smoke or drink alcohol, stopping or cutting down helps your recovery and lowers the chance of a new cancer. Ask the team for help. If you work with wood, leather or chemicals, ask about protection and tell your doctor about your work. Support is available from your dietitian, speech and swallowing therapist, dentist, counsellor and social worker. Feeling anxious or low is very common. Please tell us if you are struggling.

Questions patients often ask

Is this just a bad sinus infection?

Sinus infections are far more common. Cancer is suspected when symptoms are on one side only, keep getting worse or include bleeding, numbness or eye changes.

Will my face look different?

Reconstruction and prostheses are used to keep your appearance and function as normal as possible. Your surgeon will show you what to expect.

Will I lose my sense of smell?

It may be affected by the cancer or its treatment. Your team will discuss this before treatment.

Do I need radiotherapy?

Often yes, either alone or after surgery. The final decision depends on your pathology report.
