Mandible (Lower Jaw) Fracture Surgery

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains the operation to fix a broken lower jaw and what to expect. Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team will talk it through with you.

At a glance
  • Surgery fixes the broken bone with small titanium plates and screws.
  • It is done under a general anaesthetic, through cuts inside the mouth in most cases.
  • Wires or screws may be used to guide your bite.
  • You usually stay one night in hospital.
  • The jaw takes about six weeks to heal and you eat a soft diet during this time.
  • Numbness of the lip and chin is the commonest nerve problem and usually improves.

1. The problem and why surgery is recommended

Your lower jaw (mandible) has been broken. The number of breaks and where they are has already been checked by the doctor who examined you. Most displaced fractures heal better and with a more reliable bite if they are fixed with plates and screws. Your surgeon will have explained whether you need an operation.

One nerve runs through the centre of the lower jaw and gives feeling to the lower lip, chin and bottom teeth. It may already have been bruised by the injury.

2. The anaesthetic

The operation takes place under a general anaesthetic, so you will be fully asleep. You must not drive or operate machinery for 48 hours afterwards.

Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

3. What does the operation involve?

Once you are asleep, the fracture is opened through a cut inside your mouth in the gum. The broken bones are put back together and held with small metal plates and screws. The gum is closed with dissolvable stitches, which can take two weeks or even longer to fall out.

During the same operation, wires or small metal braces are often placed on your teeth. Elastic bands can be attached to them to guide your bite into the correct position. Screws in the jawbone above the teeth are sometimes used instead. Elastic bands are not usually attached until the day after the operation, so your jaw can move freely when you wake up.

Will anything else be done?

  • Damaged or decayed teeth at the fracture line sometimes need to be removed.
  • In very difficult fractures a cut may be needed on the skin under the jaw. If this is likely, the site and size of the scar will be discussed with you before you sign the consent form. Your surgeon will confirm which type of stitch is used for any skin cut.

4. What to expect afterwards

  • Pain. The jaw will be sore, worst in the first few days. It can take a couple of weeks to settle. Regular painkillers are provided.
  • Antibiotics. You will have them through a vein while in hospital and a course to take home.
  • Hospital stay. Usually one night. The next day, X-rays check the position of the fracture before you go home.
  • Healing. It takes about six weeks for the jaw to heal completely, even with plates. Eat a soft diet during this time. Doctors, nurses and dietitians will advise you.
  • Mouth care. Keep your mouth very clean. From the day after surgery, rinse gently with a mouthwash or warm salt water. A soft toothbrush can be used around the area as soon as you are comfortable.
  • Wires, braces or screws. If used, they are removed in the outpatient department when your doctors are happy the fracture has healed.
  • Work. Depending on your job, a fortnight or so off work is usual. Avoid strenuous exercise and contact sport.
  • Review. An appointment will be arranged before you leave hospital, and you will be followed up for several months.
Contact us if you have increasing pain or swelling, a bad taste or discharge, a fever, or your bite feels different.

5. Possible complications

Most people recover well. The risks include:

  • Pain, bruising, swelling and, if a skin cut is needed, a scar.
  • Numbness of the lower lip and chin. The nerve may be bruised by the injury or the operation. In most people it gets better by itself, though this can take several months. It is sometimes permanent.
  • Infection. This is uncommon because of the antibiotics. Published rates vary, and it can happen in a small number of patients, particularly with smoking or alcohol misuse. Infection may need further treatment and sometimes removal of a plate.
  • Bleeding. Bleeding from the cuts inside the mouth is unlikely to be a problem. If it happens at home, press for at least 10 minutes with a rolled-up handkerchief or gauze.
  • Change in bite (occlusion) or difficulty or pain when opening the mouth. This is sometimes treated with elastic bands, physiotherapy or, rarely, more surgery.
  • Damage to teeth next to the fracture from screws.
  • Fracture not healing or healing in the wrong position. This is uncommon and may need further surgery.
  • Plates and screws. They are not normally removed unless they get infected. They are made of titanium and do not set off airport metal detectors.
  • Further surgery is occasionally needed.

Questions patients often ask

Will my jaw be wired shut?

Not usually. The bands guide the bite but your mouth can still open a little.

How long until I can eat normally?

About six weeks, starting with a soft diet.

Can I fly or go through a metal detector?

Titanium plates do not set off metal detectors.
