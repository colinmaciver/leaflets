Surgery for Mouth (Oral) Cancer

Removal of the tumour, neck dissection, and microvascular reconstruction

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has surgery been recommended?

What is mouth (oral) cancer?

Mouth (oral) cancer is a cancer that develops in the tissues of the mouth — most commonly the tongue, floor of the mouth, gums, inside of the cheeks, hard palate, or lips. The great majority arise from the surface lining of the mouth, a type called squamous cell carcinoma, and, left untreated, can grow to invade deeper structures such as muscle or bone, or spread to lymph nodes in the neck.

What are the risk factors?

Mouth cancer does not have a single cause, and some patients have none of the factors below. The best-established risk factors are:

  • Tobacco use — smoking or chewing tobacco is the single biggest risk factor, and risk rises with how much and how long you have used it.
  • Alcohol — regular or heavy drinking increases risk on its own, and combining alcohol with tobacco multiplies the risk well beyond either alone.
  • Betel quid, paan, or areca nut chewing — a well-established risk factor, particularly common in parts of South Asia and the Gulf region and particularly when combined with tobacco.
  • Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection — linked more strongly to cancers of the oropharynx (the tonsils and back of the tongue) than to the mouth itself, but relevant to some oral cancers too.
  • A diet low in fruit and vegetables.
  • A weakened immune system, for example after organ transplantation or with certain medical conditions.
  • Sun exposure — a specific risk factor for cancer of the lip.
  • Increasing age — most patients are over 40, and risk continues to rise with age, though it can occur in younger people, including those with none of the factors above.
  • A pre-existing potentially precancerous patch in the mouth, such as leukoplakia (a white patch) or erythroplakia (a red patch).

Why surgery?

Surgery is the main treatment for most cancers of the mouth (oral cavity). The aim of the operation is to remove the cancer completely, along with a margin of healthy tissue around it, to give you the best chance of long-term cure and to reduce the risk of the cancer coming back.

Depending on the size and position of the tumour, and whether there is any risk of spread, your surgical team may recommend one, two, or all three of the following during the same operation:

  • Resection of the tumour – removing the cancer from the mouth or jaw.
  • Neck dissection – removing lymph nodes in the neck that may contain, or be at risk of containing, cancer cells.
  • Microvascular (free flap) reconstruction – rebuilding the area that has been removed, using tissue moved from elsewhere in the body, to restore form and function.

Your case will have been discussed by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) of head and neck surgeons, oncologists, radiologists, pathologists and specialist nurses, who agree together on the treatment that gives you the best possible outcome. Some patients also need radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy after surgery, which your team will discuss with you separately.

In some cases, surgery and radiotherapy offer similarly good outcomes for the same cancer. Where this applies to you, your team should explain both options clearly, including their likely side effects, so that you can make an informed choice based on what matters most to you.

2. The anaesthetic

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable.

Combined resection, neck dissection and free flap reconstruction is major surgery and commonly takes between six and twelve hours, depending on complexity. A longer combined operation carries a somewhat higher overall anaesthetic risk than a single-site procedure, which your anaesthetist will discuss with you as part of your assessment.

3. What does the operation involve?

Resection of the tumour

The surgeon removes the visible and palpable tumour together with a rim of surrounding healthy-looking tissue (a 'margin'), to reduce the chance of leaving cancer cells behind. The exact approach depends on where the tumour is:

  • Small tumours on the tongue, floor of mouth or cheek can sometimes be removed through the mouth.
  • Larger tumours, or those close to or involving the jawbone, may need an incision in the neck or lip, and occasionally the lower jaw (mandible) is divided and later rejoined with a small plate simply to give the surgeon better access.
  • If the tumour is close to the jawbone but has not grown into it, a marginal mandibulectomy may be performed — a rim of bone is removed together with the tumour, leaving the jaw continuous.
  • If the cancer has grown into the bone itself, a segmental resection is needed — a full section of the jawbone is removed with the tumour, and the jaw is rejoined at the end of the operation using titanium plates (often together with bone reconstruction, described below).

The removed tissue is sent to a specialist pathologist, who examines it under the microscope to confirm the type of cancer, how far it has spread, and whether the margins are clear of tumour cells. These results usually take one to two weeks and will be explained to you at a follow-up appointment.

Neck dissection

Oral cancers can spread to lymph nodes in the neck before they cause any other symptoms. A neck dissection removes lymph nodes (and sometimes the fatty tissue surrounding them) from one or both sides of the neck through an incision in a natural skin crease. See the separate Neck Dissection leaflet for full detail.

  • Selective neck dissection removes specific groups of lymph nodes most at risk, while preserving important nerves, muscles and blood vessels wherever possible.
  • More extensive neck dissections are occasionally needed if nodes are clearly involved with cancer, and may involve removing structures such as a vein, muscle or nerve if the cancer has grown into them.
  • A drain (a fine tube attached to a small suction bottle) is usually left in the neck for a few days to remove excess fluid while the area heals.

Microvascular (free flap) reconstruction

When a significant amount of tissue is removed, reconstruction is carried out during the same operation to rebuild the mouth, tongue, jaw or facial contour. This helps to restore your ability to speak, swallow and eat, and to support your facial appearance.

Microvascular, or 'free flap', reconstruction involves transferring a piece of tissue — including its own blood vessels — from another part of your body to the head and neck. A second surgical team works at the donor site at the same time as the tumour is being removed, so the two parts of the operation often run in parallel to keep total surgery time as short as possible.

  • Soft tissue free flaps rebuild areas such as the tongue, floor of mouth or cheek lining, commonly using tissue from the forearm (radial forearm flap), thigh (anterolateral thigh flap) or shoulder blade region (scapular flap).
  • Bony free flaps are used when part of the jaw has been removed. The lower leg (fibula) is the most common source, as it provides a long, strong piece of bone — along with skin if needed — that can be carefully shaped to rebuild the jaw.
  • Under surgical loupe magnification, the tiny blood vessels of the transferred tissue are carefully joined to blood vessels in the neck, so the flap develops its own blood supply in its new position.
  • Once the flap is connected, its blood flow is monitored very closely, usually with hourly checks for the first few days, since a healthy blood supply is essential for the flap to survive.

Reconstruction is tailored to what has been removed — this may mean rebuilding soft tissue only, or bone and soft tissue together if part of the jaw has been taken. Your surgeon will explain which donor site is planned for you and why.

4. What to expect afterwards

Immediately afterwards

  • Most patients spend the first one to two nights in a high-dependency or intensive care unit for close monitoring, followed by seven to fourteen days on the ward, though this varies from person to person.
  • A feeding tube (through the nose, or sometimes directly into the stomach) is commonly used for a period after surgery while the mouth heals, and a speech and language therapist will guide your return to swallowing and eating.
  • Some patients need a temporary tracheostomy (a breathing tube through the neck) for a few days after larger operations, to keep the airway safe while swelling settles. See the separate Tracheostomy leaflet for detail. It is removed as soon as it is no longer needed.
  • You will be cared for by a multidisciplinary team including your surgeons, specialist nurses, speech and language therapists, dietitians, and physiotherapists, who will support your recovery, communication and nutrition throughout your stay.

As you recover

  • Swelling and bruising in the mouth, neck and any donor site are normal in the first two to three weeks.
  • Speech and swallowing often improve steadily over weeks to months, supported by a speech and language therapist. Some patients notice permanent changes in speech clarity or swallowing depending on how much tissue was removed.
  • Return to a normal diet is usually gradual, starting with soft or pureed foods and building up as healing progresses and swallowing is reassessed.
  • Most people are able to return to light daily activities within four to six weeks, with fuller recovery, including return to work, often taking two to three months.
  • Facial appearance and jaw shape are supported by the reconstruction, though some change in contour, symmetry or sensation is expected.
  • Regular follow-up appointments continue for several years to check healing, monitor for recurrence, and support your ongoing rehabilitation.

5. Possible risks and complications

As with any major surgery, there are risks, but the great majority of patients recover well and your surgical team monitors closely for these and has clear plans in place to manage them. Your surgeon will discuss those most relevant to your operation, but general categories include:

General surgical risks

  • Bleeding or bruising.
  • Infection of the wound or chest. Wound-healing problems of some kind (most of them minor) are reported in about 1 in 3 to 1 in 2 patients after major surgery with a free flap, chest complications in roughly 1 in 4, and pneumonia specifically in roughly 5 in 100 (about 1 in 9 when a free flap is used).
  • Blood clots in the leg (DVT) or lung (pulmonary embolism).
  • Risks associated with a general anaesthetic.
  • Scarring.

Risks specific to tumour resection

  • Changes to speech, chewing or swallowing, depending on how much tissue is removed.
  • Numbness or altered sensation in the mouth, lip or chin.
  • If the pathology results show the margins are not fully clear, further (revision) surgery may be needed. See below for how this is decided and communicated.

Risks specific to neck dissection

  • Numbness of the neck, ear or shoulder skin.
  • Shoulder stiffness or weakness if a nerve supplying the shoulder muscle is affected or needs to be removed. Some shoulder symptoms are reported in roughly 3 to 7 in 10 patients after neck dissection, often settling with physiotherapy.
  • Fluid collection under the skin (seroma) or leakage of lymphatic fluid (a chyle leak, reported in roughly 1 to 6 in 100 patients).
  • Weakness of the lower lip if a nerve near the jawline is affected.

Risks specific to microvascular reconstruction

  • Flap failure — a free flap loses its blood supply and does not survive. This occurs in about 5 in 100 cases (around 5%; published figures range from about 3 to 6 in 100), and may require a return to theatre or use of an alternative reconstruction. Overall, roughly 1 in 5 patients having this type of surgery needs an unplanned return to theatre for any reason, most often for a flap blood-supply problem, infection or bleeding.
  • Problems at the donor site, such as delayed healing, altered sensation, or (for leg or forearm donor sites) temporary changes in strength or movement.
  • A longer combined operation carries a higher overall anaesthetic risk than a single-site procedure.

Unexpected findings

Different or more extensive pathology than expected — including margins that are not completely clear — is possible once the tissue has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan — which may include further surgery, radiotherapy, or a combination of treatments — and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead, usually once results are available around one to two weeks after surgery.

Please raise any worries with your team — they are used to these questions and would rather you ask.

Questions patients often ask

Will I definitely need a neck dissection?

Not always. This depends on the type, size and depth of your tumour, and the risk of the cancer having spread to lymph nodes. Your surgeon will explain whether this applies to you and, if so, which side(s) of the neck are involved.

Why do I need tissue taken from another part of my body?

When cancer removal leaves a gap too large to close directly, or removes structures such as the jawbone, tissue from elsewhere with its own blood supply is used to rebuild the area. This restores form and function far better than simply closing the wound.

Will the reconstruction affect the donor site long-term?

Most donor sites heal well with a linear scar and only minor changes in sensation or function. Your surgeon will tell you which donor site is planned and any specific effects to expect, such as temporary weakness after a leg or forearm flap.

Will I be able to speak and eat normally afterwards?

Many patients return to a good standard of speech and a near-normal diet, particularly after smaller resections. Larger resections can affect speech clarity or swallowing, and a speech and language therapist will work with you before and after surgery to support the best possible recovery.

How long will I be in hospital?

Typically one to two weeks, including a short initial period of closer monitoring, though this varies depending on the extent of surgery and your recovery.

Will I need a tracheostomy or feeding tube?

Many, but not all, patients having combined resection, neck dissection and reconstruction need a temporary tracheostomy and feeding tube for the first days after surgery. Both are usually removed once swelling settles and swallowing is safe.

What if the pathology results show the cancer wasn't fully removed?

If the margins are not completely clear, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting — a group of specialists who together agree the most appropriate plan, which may include further surgery, radiotherapy, or a combination of treatments — and this will then be explained to you in person, usually once results are available one to two weeks after surgery.

Will I need radiotherapy as well as surgery?

This decision is based on the pathology results after surgery, including tumour size, spread to lymph nodes, and margin status. Not everyone needs radiotherapy. Your case will be discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, and the recommended plan — with the reasons for it — will then be explained to you in person.

How will the surgery affect my appearance?

Reconstruction is planned to support your facial shape and contour as closely as possible, but some change in appearance, symmetry, swelling or sensation should be expected. Your team can discuss realistic expectations for your specific operation.

When can I go back to work and normal activities?

This varies with the extent of surgery and the physical demands of your role, but many patients are ready for light activities within four to six weeks and fuller activity by two to three months. Your team will advise on timing for your specific circumstances.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, or any change in the colour or warmth of your flap or wound. Use the contact details below.
