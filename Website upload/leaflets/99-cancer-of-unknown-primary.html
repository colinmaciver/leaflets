Cancer of Unknown Primary (Neck Lump With No Obvious Source)

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains what it means when a cancer is found in a lump in the neck but the place it started cannot be found. It is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team. Your own plan will be agreed with you in person.

At a glance
  • Cancer of unknown primary means cancer has been found in a neck lymph node, but tests have not yet found where it started.
  • It accounts for about 3 to 5 in 100 head and neck cancers.
  • The usual first sign is a painless lump in the neck.
  • The hidden starting point is often small and in the tonsil or the back of the tongue. A special examination under anaesthetic may find it.
  • It is treated with surgery, radiotherapy or both, with chemotherapy for some people. Many people are cured.

1. What is cancer of unknown primary?

Lymph nodes are small bean-shaped glands that are part of the body's defence against infection. Hundreds lie in the head and neck, mostly down the sides of the neck and under the jaw. They are normally soft and small and hard to feel. They can swell because of infection or, less often, because cancer has spread to them.

Cancer of unknown primary (CUP) in the head and neck means cancer cells have been found in a neck lymph node, but the first cancer (the 'primary') cannot be found, even after tests. It accounts for about 3 to 5 in 100 (3 to 5%) of all head and neck cancers. The primary is often very small, can be hidden in folds of the tonsil or the back of the tongue, and may be too small to see or show on scans.

Most cancers that spread to neck nodes start in the flat lining cells of the mouth, nose and throat (squamous cell cancers). A few start in the salivary glands or the thyroid, and some come from skin cancers.

What causes it?

  • Smoking and alcohol, particularly together.
  • HPV (human papillomavirus): many hidden cancers in the tonsil or tongue base are caused by HPV. See our leaflet on HPV-related throat cancer.
  • Epstein-Barr virus, in some cases, from the back of the nose.
  • Sun exposure and skin cancers, which can spread to nodes on the face, scalp and neck.

Possible symptoms

The usual sign is a painless lump in the neck. Most people have no other symptoms. A swollen node from an infection should settle within about two weeks. In an adult, a neck lump that has not gone away within 4 weeks should be checked by a doctor, and a needle test is usually advised.

2. How is it diagnosed?

The aim is to confirm the type of cancer and to try to find where it came from. Your surgeon will ask about your history, symptoms, smoking, alcohol, previous skin cancers and examine your mouth, throat, nose, thyroid, skin and neck. Not everyone needs every test.

  • Needle biopsy (FNA) of the lump, guided by ultrasound with local anaesthetic. A pathologist checks the cells. If it is not clear, the test may be repeated or a larger core biopsy is done. Occasionally a surgeon removes a whole node (excision biopsy) under general anaesthetic. Special tests on the sample, such as for HPV and Epstein-Barr virus, can point to where the cancer started.
  • Ultrasound of the neck and thyroid.
  • CT and MRI scans of the head and neck, sometimes the chest too.
  • PET-CT scan: uses a mildly radioactive form of sugar to look for the primary and for spread elsewhere. It can show hidden primaries that other scans miss.
  • Nasoendoscopy: a thin flexible camera passed through the nose to inspect the nose and throat.
  • Examination under anaesthetic (EUA) with biopsies: the mouth, throat, voice box and back of the nose are carefully examined while you are asleep, and samples are taken from likely places, which can include removing the tonsils (tonsillectomy) and samples from the back of the tongue. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

3. Your specialist team

Some tests may not find the primary. This does not stop treatment. Your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

The team includes head and neck surgeons, reconstructive surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, oncologists (cancer doctors who give radiotherapy and chemotherapy), specialist nurses, speech and swallowing therapists, dietitians, dental and oral medicine specialists, and psychological and social support. You are always welcome to ask for a second opinion.

4. Treatment options

The best treatment depends on the number and size of the affected nodes, whether a primary has been found, your general health and your wishes. Options are surgery, radiotherapy, or both, with chemotherapy added in some people.

Surgery

Surgery is done under general anaesthetic. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

  • Neck dissection: removal of the enlarged lymph nodes together with other nodes in the same region of the neck. Possible effects include neck and ear numbness, shoulder stiffness or weakness, neck swelling for a time, and a scar. Physiotherapy exercises help.
  • Tonsillectomy: removal of the tonsils if cancer is suspected there. It can also show the type of cancer.

A small drain is occasionally used and stitches are dissolvable. Your surgeon will tell you how long you will stay in hospital.

Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is given from outside the body, daily on weekdays. It can be used on its own for about 7 weeks, or after surgery, starting about 4 weeks later and lasting around 6 weeks. It can target only the affected side of the neck, or both sides of the throat to treat all the places the primary might be hiding. Chemotherapy is sometimes added (chemoradiotherapy) to make it work better, with more side effects.

Side effects can include:

  • tiredness
  • sore, red or dry skin in the treated area
  • a sore mouth and throat, and pain on swallowing
  • dry mouth and thickened saliva
  • changes in taste, and a loss of appetite and weight
  • a hoarse voice

Most side effects start around two weeks into treatment, are at their worst just after it finishes and improve over the following 4 to 6 weeks. Some can last for months and a few may be permanent, such as a dry mouth. Because radiotherapy can affect the jaw bone, any broken or infected teeth are usually treated before it starts, and regular dental check-ups afterwards are important. About 12 weeks after radiotherapy a PET scan is usually done to check the cancer has gone. If it has not, or if it comes back, surgery may still be possible.

5. Follow-up and outlook

After treatment you will have regular check-ups to make sure the cancer in the nodes has not returned and to see whether the original cancer ever appears. This includes examination and camera checks of the nose and throat, and sometimes CT, MRI or PET scans.

Not knowing where a cancer started can be worrying. In practice, treating the neck and the likely hidden sites with surgery and radiotherapy gives many people a good chance of cure. The outlook depends on the type of cancer, how many nodes are involved, whether HPV is the cause and your general health. Your team will talk through what this means for you in a personal and honest conversation.

If you smoke or drink alcohol, stopping or cutting down helps your recovery and lowers the chance of a new cancer. Ask the team for help. Support is available from your dietitian, speech and swallowing therapist, dentist, counsellor and social worker. Feeling anxious or low is very common. Please tell us if you are struggling.

Questions patients often ask

Why can't you find where it came from?

The first cancer can be tiny, can hide in the tonsil or tongue base, or may even have been cleared by your body. Not finding it does not change that the node cancer needs treating.

Does this mean it is worse?

Not necessarily. Many hidden cancers are HPV-related and respond well to treatment.

Will I have to have my tonsils out?

Possibly. If a primary cancer might be hiding there, removing the tonsils can find it and treat it.

Could the primary appear later?

Occasionally. That is part of why regular follow-up is important.
