Sublingual Gland Excision

Surgery to remove a sublingual salivary gland, usually for a ranula

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

At a glance

  • What happens during the operation, including the anaesthetic

1. Why has surgery been recommended?

Your sublingual glands are two small salivary glands that sit under the tongue, on the floor of the mouth. They produce saliva that drains directly into the mouth through several tiny openings.

The most common reason for removing a sublingual gland is a ranula: a soft, fluid-filled swelling on the floor of the mouth that forms when saliva leaks from the gland or its ducts into the surrounding tissue instead of draining normally into the mouth. A ranula is not a cancer, but it tends to keep refilling and causing discomfort, particularly with eating and speaking, if the gland producing it is not removed.

Occasionally surgery is recommended for other sublingual gland problems, such as a persistent lump or a gland that keeps becoming inflamed. Removing the gland resolves the underlying problem, rather than just draining the swelling, which tends to recur if the gland is left behind.

2. What does the procedure involve?

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. It is a relatively short procedure, typically taking around 30 to 45 minutes. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable.

Nearby structures

Two structures running close to the sublingual gland need to be carefully identified and protected during surgery: the lingual nerve, which supplies feeling and taste to the side of the tongue, and the submandibular duct, which drains a different salivary gland (the submandibular gland) into the mouth and runs directly alongside the sublingual gland.

The procedure

  • The submandibular duct is identified and a fine tube is passed into it to help the surgeon see and protect it throughout the operation.
  • An incision is made in the lining of the mouth over the floor of the mouth, and the sublingual gland (and the ranula, if present) is carefully dissected away from the surrounding tissue.
  • The lingual nerve and submandibular duct are identified and preserved throughout, working closely around them to keep them intact.
  • The gland and any ranula are removed, bleeding is carefully controlled, and the lining of the mouth is closed with dissolvable stitches.

3. What is the expected outcome?

Immediately afterwards

  • This is usually a day-case procedure, meaning most patients go home the same day.
  • You will be given antibiotics to continue for about a week, and regular pain relief.
  • A pureed diet is recommended for the first 48 hours while the floor of the mouth settles, moving on to a soft diet as comfort allows.
  • Keeping the mouth very clean is important while it heals — you will be given a chlorhexidine mouthwash to use for about a week.

As you recover

  • Some swelling and discomfort under the tongue is normal for the first week or two and gradually settles.
  • Any numbness or altered taste on the side of the tongue, if it occurs, usually improves over the following weeks to months as the lingual nerve settles after being handled during surgery.
  • A follow-up appointment is arranged to check healing and discuss the findings from the gland once it has been examined.

4. Possible risks and complications

As with any surgery, there are risks. Your surgeon will discuss those most relevant to you, but general categories include:

  • Bleeding or a collection of blood (haematoma) under the tongue — occasionally significant enough to affect the airway in the first hours after surgery, which is why you will be monitored closely afterwards.
  • Infection of the wound, which is uncommon and usually settles with antibiotics.
  • Altered sensation or taste on the side of the tongue — usually temporary due to handling of the lingual nerve during surgery, though a small risk of permanent change exists.
  • Injury to the submandibular duct, which in rare cases can lead to swelling of the submandibular gland afterwards, as it no longer drains as freely.
  • Recurrence of a ranula, which is uncommon once the sublingual gland itself has been removed, but is more likely if any gland tissue is left behind.
  • Temporary swelling of the floor of the mouth affecting speech, eating or the fit of any dentures, which settles as the swelling goes down.
Your surgical team monitors closely for these complications and has clear plans in place to manage them if they occur. Please raise any worries with your team — they are used to these questions and would rather you ask.

Questions patients often ask

Will my speech or eating be affected long-term?

No. Any swelling or altered sensation in the early weeks settles as the area heals, and normal speech and eating return as the floor of the mouth recovers.

Will the ranula come back?

Removing the sublingual gland itself, rather than just draining the ranula, is specifically intended to stop it recurring, and recurrence is uncommon once the gland has been fully removed.

Why is a tube passed into the submandibular duct during surgery?

It helps the surgical team see exactly where the duct runs so it can be protected throughout the operation, since it sits directly next to the sublingual gland.

Will I have a visible scar?

No. The incision is made inside the mouth, on the floor of the mouth under the tongue, so there is no visible external scar.

How long will I be in hospital?

This is usually a day-case procedure, so most patients go home the same day.

When can I go back to a normal diet?

A pureed diet is recommended for the first 48 hours, moving on to a soft diet as comfort allows, and back to a normal diet over the following one to two weeks.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice significant swelling under the tongue, difficulty breathing or swallowing, bleeding, or fever. Use the contact details below.
