Subtotal Parathyroidectomy for Renal Hyperparathyroidism

Surgery to remove three and a half overactive parathyroid glands in kidney-related (secondary or tertiary) hyperparathyroidism

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

At a glance
  • What renal (secondary/tertiary) hyperparathyroidism is and why surgery is recommended
  • What happens during the operation, including the anaesthetic
  • What outcome to expect, immediately afterwards and as you recover

1. Why has surgery been recommended?

You normally have four parathyroid glands in the neck, which control your blood calcium level by producing parathyroid hormone (PTH). In long-standing kidney disease, the kidneys lose the ability to properly regulate calcium, vitamin D and phosphate, and all four parathyroid glands can respond by enlarging and overproducing PTH — this is called secondary hyperparathyroidism. If this continues even after a kidney transplant, or becomes self-sustaining and excessive, it is called tertiary hyperparathyroidism.

Unlike the single overactive gland seen in primary hyperparathyroidism, this condition typically affects all four glands. When it cannot be controlled with medication (such as vitamin D analogues, phosphate binders, or calcimimetics) and is causing problems such as bone pain, fractures, severe itching, or calcium deposits in blood vessels and soft tissue, surgery to remove most of the overactive parathyroid tissue is recommended.

What the operation involves

Because all four glands are typically involved, this operation explores both sides of the neck to find all four parathyroid glands, rather than the focused, single-sided approach used for a single adenoma. Three glands and most of the fourth are removed (a subtotal, or '3½ gland', parathyroidectomy), leaving a small, carefully chosen and well-blooded remnant of parathyroid tissue to continue producing some PTH, which helps avoid needing lifelong calcium supplements. An alternative approach, total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation, removes all visible parathyroid tissue and reimplants a small piece into a muscle (usually in the forearm or neck) — your surgeon will discuss which approach is planned for you and why.

2. The anaesthetic

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout, and typically takes around one and a half to two and a half hours, reflecting the need to explore and identify all four glands. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. This is particularly important given your kidney disease. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable, and the timing of your operation is usually coordinated with your renal (kidney) team, including around your dialysis schedule if you are on dialysis.

3. What does the procedure involve?

  • An incision is made in a natural crease in the middle of the lower neck, positioned to keep the resulting scar as inconspicuous as possible.
  • The strap muscles overlying the thyroid are divided in the midline, and both sides of the thyroid capsule are carefully dissected to expose all four parathyroid glands, working close to protect the recurrent laryngeal nerves on each side.
  • All four parathyroid glands are identified, and their size and appearance assessed to confirm the diagnosis.
  • Three glands, and most of the fourth, are removed, leaving a small, well-blooded remnant of parathyroid tissue in the neck (or, if a total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation is planned instead, all visible parathyroid tissue is removed and a small piece reimplanted into a muscle).
  • The remaining parathyroid remnant is usually marked with a small clip or suture so it can be identified again in future if needed.
  • Bleeding is carefully controlled, a small drain is often left beneath the skin given the more extensive dissection, and the wound is closed in layers with dissolvable stitches.

The recurrent laryngeal nerves, which control the voice box, run close to the thyroid and parathyroid glands on both sides of the neck and are carefully identified and protected throughout the operation.

4. What to expect afterwards

Immediately afterwards

  • Most patients stay in hospital for a day or two, partly to allow close monitoring of your blood calcium level, which can fall significantly and needs careful management.
  • Your blood calcium is checked frequently in the first 24 to 48 hours, and calcium (sometimes given intravenously in the first day or two) and vitamin D supplements are usually started promptly.
  • If a drain has been used, it is usually removed after a day or two once drainage has settled.
  • You will be given regular pain relief, and your renal team will be involved in your care alongside the surgical team, including planning around any dialysis sessions.

As you recover

  • Swelling and bruising in the neck generally settle within one to two weeks, and the scar continues to fade over several months.
  • Calcium and vitamin D (and sometimes calcitriol) supplements are usually needed for some weeks to months afterwards while your bones, which have often been depleted of calcium by long-standing overactive parathyroid hormone, take up calcium again — this is called 'hungry bone syndrome' and is common after this operation (roughly 2 to 4 in 10 patients), more so than after surgery for a single adenoma.
  • Regular blood tests are needed in the weeks after surgery to fine-tune your calcium and vitamin D supplements as your levels stabilise.
  • A follow-up appointment is arranged with your surgical and renal teams together to review your progress and blood results.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications are possible, but the great majority of patients tolerate this operation well, and most complications are manageable with close monitoring. The approximate figures below are taken from published studies of large numbers of patients, so you can make an informed decision — your own surgeon will discuss which are most relevant to your individual case, taking your kidney disease and general health into account:

  • Bleeding or a collection of blood under the skin (a haematoma) — uncommon, but this is watched for closely given your operation involves both sides of the neck.
  • Temporary voice change or hoarseness, from handling of the recurrent laryngeal nerve — this is a bilateral operation, so the risk is somewhat higher than for a single-sided procedure; permanent voice change is uncommon, affecting around 1 in 100 patients (1%) or slightly more after exploring both sides.
  • Hungry bone syndrome — a marked, sometimes prolonged drop in blood calcium as your bones take up calcium again after years of overactive parathyroid hormone. This is common after this operation, reported in roughly 2 to 4 in 10 patients (about 20 to 35%) in recent studies, and more often when bone disease was severe beforehand, and is managed with calcium and vitamin D supplements, sometimes including intravenous calcium in the first few days.
  • Recurrence of overactive parathyroid tissue over time, either from the deliberately left remnant or from tissue elsewhere in the neck or chest — occurs in roughly 1 in 10 to 1 in 5 patients (about 10 to 25%) over the years after surgery, depending on follow-up length, and may need further treatment if it happens.
  • Persistently low or persistently high calcium requiring longer-term medication adjustment — your renal and surgical teams will monitor this closely together.
  • Wound infection — uncommon and usually settles with antibiotics.
  • Different pathology than expected on final examination — very rarely, tissue removed shows unexpected features on detailed examination. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

Questions patients often ask

Why are all four glands involved, unlike some other patients having parathyroid surgery?

In kidney-related (renal) hyperparathyroidism, long-standing changes in calcium, vitamin D and phosphate affect all four parathyroid glands rather than just one, which is why this operation explores both sides of the neck rather than focusing on a single gland.

Why is my calcium likely to drop so much afterwards?

This is called hungry bone syndrome. Long-standing high parathyroid hormone levels can deplete calcium from your bones; once the overactive glands are removed, your bones take up calcium rapidly from your bloodstream, causing your blood calcium to fall, sometimes significantly, for some weeks. This is expected and is closely monitored and treated with calcium and vitamin D supplements.

Will I need calcium supplements for life?

Many patients need supplements for weeks to months while their bones and remaining parathyroid remnant settle, and some go on to need longer-term or lifelong supplementation, particularly if the parathyroid remnant does not provide enough hormone. Your levels will be monitored regularly with your renal team to guide this.

Could this come back again in the future?

Yes, recurrence is possible, occurring in roughly 1 in 10 to 1 in 5 patients over time, either from the small remnant left behind or from parathyroid tissue elsewhere. This is monitored with regular blood tests, and further treatment can be arranged if it happens.

Will my voice be affected?

A temporary voice change is possible given both sides of the neck are explored, and it usually settles. Permanent voice change is uncommon, affecting around 1 in 100 patients (1%) or slightly more after exploring both sides.

How long will I be in hospital?

Most patients stay a day or two, partly to allow close monitoring and treatment of your calcium level in the days after surgery.

Will I have a visible scar?

The incision is placed in a natural crease in the lower neck specifically to keep it as inconspicuous as possible, and it typically fades well over the following months.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, voice change, or tingling around your lips, fingertips or toes (a possible sign of low calcium). Your renal team should also be informed of any of these symptoms given your kidney disease. Use the contact details below.
