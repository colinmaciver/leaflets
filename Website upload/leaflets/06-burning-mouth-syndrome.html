Burning Mouth Syndrome

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains burning mouth syndrome, a long-term condition that causes a burning feeling in a healthy-looking mouth. Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team will support you in managing it.

At a glance
  • Burning mouth syndrome causes a burning, scalding or tingling feeling, most often on the tongue, lips or roof of the mouth.
  • The mouth looks normal. The cause is thought to be a problem with the nerves that carry pain and taste.
  • It is most common in women around and after the menopause.
  • Other causes of a sore mouth need to be ruled out first, such as vitamin deficiency, thrush and denture problems.
  • It is not cancer and not infectious. It often improves with treatment and time.

1. What is burning mouth syndrome and why does it happen?

Burning mouth syndrome (BMS) is a long-lasting burning or scalded feeling in the mouth. It is most often on the tip and sides of the tongue, but may affect the lips, gums or roof of the mouth. The mouth looks normal when examined, and there are no ulcers or patches that explain the pain.

Some people also notice a dry mouth, a bitter or metallic taste, or a change in taste. The burning is often mild in the morning, then builds up through the day and is worst in the evening. It often eases while eating or drinking. It affects women more than men, mostly in middle age and later, and is common around the menopause.

What causes it?

In most people no single cause is found. This is called primary burning mouth syndrome. It is thought to come from changes in how the small nerves of the mouth and the brain's pain pathways work, so that pain signals are sent even though there is no injury. Hormone changes, stress, anxiety and low mood may play a part and can make it worse, but they are not the whole explanation. You are not imagining it.

In some people, burning is caused by another problem. This is called secondary burning mouth. Examples include:

  • Low iron, vitamin B12, folate or other vitamin or mineral levels
  • Diabetes
  • Thrush (a fungal infection), which is more likely with dentures, a dry mouth or after antibiotics or steroid inhalers
  • Ill-fitting dentures, or a sore from a sharp tooth
  • Allergy or irritation from toothpaste, mouthwash, food flavourings or dental materials
  • Dry mouth, caused by medicines or other conditions
  • Some medicines, including some blood pressure tablets (ACE inhibitors)
  • Lichen planus or other skin conditions of the mouth
  • Habits such as tongue thrusting or clenching and grinding

2. How is it assessed?

Your surgeon will ask about the burning, when it happens, what makes it better or worse, any taste change and dry mouth, your medicines, dentures and general health. They will examine your mouth, teeth, dentures and tongue.

Blood tests are often done to check for diabetes and for low levels of iron, vitamin B12, folate and other vitamins. A swab of the mouth may check for thrush. Occasionally, an allergy test or a biopsy of the mouth lining is needed. If these tests are all normal and no cause is found, the diagnosis of primary burning mouth syndrome is made.

It can be reassuring to know that the condition is not cancer and does not lead to cancer.

3. Self-care and treatment

What can help yourself

  • Avoid things that irritate the mouth: spicy, acidic or very hot food and drink, alcohol and alcohol-based mouthwash, mint and cinnamon flavours, and tobacco.
  • Switch to a mild, flavour-free or SLS-free toothpaste.
  • Sip cool water, suck ice chips or chew sugar-free gum to ease the burning.
  • Keep your mouth moist if it is dry. Saliva substitutes can help.
  • Keep your dentures clean, take them out at night, and see your dentist if they do not fit well.
  • Try to reduce stress, and find ways to relax. Keep a diary of what makes it worse or better.
  • Do not poke or rub the area with your tongue.

Treating a cause

If a cause is found, treating it often brings relief. Examples are replacing a vitamin or iron, treating thrush, adjusting dentures or changing a medicine.

Medicines for primary burning mouth syndrome

If nothing else is found and the burning is troublesome, a specialist may suggest one of the following. They work on the nerves and are used in lower doses than for other conditions.

  • Clonazepam. This is used as a lozenge that is sucked and spat out, or as a tablet. It can cause drowsiness, and care is needed with long-term use.
  • Low-dose nerve pain medicines such as amitriptyline, nortriptyline, gabapentin or pregabalin. These may take several weeks to work and can cause drowsiness or dry mouth.
  • Capsaicin mouth rinses or alpha-lipoic acid. Evidence for these is mixed.

No one treatment works for everyone. It may take some trial and error to find the best option for you.

Talking therapies

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and other talking therapies can help people cope with long-term pain and have been shown to reduce symptoms in some people.

4. What is the outlook?

Burning mouth syndrome can last for months or years. For some people it improves or goes away by itself, often within a few years, and many people find that symptoms ease with treatment and support. Others have symptoms that come and go for a long time. It does not damage your mouth, and it does not lead to cancer.

Please let us know if you notice an ulcer or a lump or patch in your mouth that lasts more than 3 weeks, or if you have swelling, bleeding, a change in feeling or trouble swallowing. These are not part of burning mouth syndrome and should be checked.

Questions patients often ask

Is burning mouth syndrome a sign of cancer?

No. The mouth looks normal and the condition is not linked to cancer. We check your mouth carefully to be sure.

Is it contagious?

No. You cannot pass it on to anyone.

Will it go away?

Often it eases over time, and treatment helps many people, but it may take months or longer.

Is it all in my head?

No. The burning is real. It is caused by changes in how the nerves send pain signals, even though nothing looks wrong.

Should I stop my tablets?

Please do not stop any medicine without advice. If one might be causing it, we will talk to your doctor.
