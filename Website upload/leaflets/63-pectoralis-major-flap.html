Pectoralis Major Flap

Using muscle and skin from your chest, kept on its own blood supply, to rebuild an area after head and neck surgery

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has this been recommended?

The pectoralis major (often shortened to 'pec major') is a thick, fan-shaped muscle at the upper front of the chest wall — it makes up the bulk of the chest muscles in men and lies beneath the breast in women. A pectoralis major flap uses a piece of this muscle, together with an overlying paddle of skin, to rebuild an area of the head or neck after tissue has been removed, particularly after surgery for cancers of the neck, throat, or mouth.

Unlike the free flaps described in some of our other reconstruction leaflets, this flap is not completely disconnected and reattached using loupe magnification. Instead, it keeps its own blood supply throughout and is simply tunnelled under the skin from the chest up to the head and neck. This makes it a reliable, technically more straightforward option with an excellent blood supply, and it gives a good functional and cosmetic result — it remains one of the most commonly used methods of reconstruction in head and neck surgery.

2. What does the anaesthetic involve?

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable. Combining reconstruction with resection of the original problem is major surgery and commonly takes several hours, and many patients spend time in a high-dependency unit afterwards for close monitoring.

3. What does the procedure involve?

  • A piece of skin and underlying pectoralis major muscle is marked out and raised from the chest, keeping its blood vessels attached throughout.
  • The flap, still connected to its own blood supply, is tunnelled beneath the skin from the chest up to the head and neck.
  • The flap is shaped and inset (stitched into place) at the defect in the head or neck.
  • The donor site on your chest is closed directly with dissolvable stitches.
  • A drain is placed at the donor site to remove any excess blood or fluid, and is monitored regularly until it is safe to remove.

4. What to expect afterwards

Immediately afterwards

  • Your chest will be covered with a protective dressing, which is checked regularly by the nursing staff and is usually changed after a couple of days once the wound has settled.
  • The donor site drain stays in place until it is producing very little fluid, at which point it is removed by the nursing staff.
  • You may notice some swelling in your neck where the flap was tunnelled through — this settles over time, and in the meantime some patients find a scarf or high-collared clothing helpful if they would prefer to disguise it.

As you recover

  • Movement of the arm on the side the flap was taken from is often uncomfortable in the first few days, and regular painkillers are provided to help with this. Only gentle arm movement is recommended initially, and your physiotherapist will guide you through an appropriate exercise plan afterwards.
  • The chest wound and stitches settle and dissolve over the following weeks.
  • The operation leaves a scar on the chest, and depending on where the flap is taken from, the position of the breast or nipple can be slightly altered. The scar fades and becomes less visible over time, and camouflage make-up support is available if this is a concern to you.
  • Your progress is followed up regularly, and if this reconstruction was combined with cancer surgery, a follow-up appointment is arranged to discuss the pathology results and any further treatment needed.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications are possible, but the great majority of patients recover well from this operation, and your team monitors closely for these and has clear plans to manage them:

  • Pain, swelling, bleeding, and infection — expected to some degree at both the reconstructed site and the chest donor site, and settling with simple measures if they occur.
  • A build-up of fluid at the donor site after the drain is removed (a seroma), which can usually be managed simply by drawing the fluid off with a fine needle in clinic.
  • Blood vessel problems affecting the flap — in around 2 to 3% of cases, one of the blood vessels supplying or draining the flap develops a clot, meaning the flap does not receive fresh blood, or becomes congested if the draining vein is affected. This usually becomes apparent within the first two days, and means an urgent return to theatre to try to clear the clot. This is not always successful, and if the flap cannot be saved, an alternative method of reconstruction is used instead.
  • A visible scar on the chest, and, depending on how much tissue is needed, some alteration in the position or shape of the breast or nipple.
  • General risks of a longer operation and anaesthetic, including chest infection and blood clots in the leg (DVT) or lung (pulmonary embolism), which your anaesthetic assessment will take into account.
  • Further surgery may occasionally be needed to manage a complication or adjust the flap's shape or bulk.
If this reconstruction is combined with cancer surgery, the pathology may occasionally be different or more extensive than expected once the tissue has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

Questions patients often ask

How is this different from a free flap?

A pectoralis major flap keeps its own blood supply the whole time and is simply tunnelled under the skin to reach the head and neck, rather than being completely disconnected and rejoined to new blood vessels using loupe magnification, as happens with a free flap. This makes it a more straightforward operation with a very reliable blood supply.

Will I have a visible scar on my chest?

Yes, a scar remains where the flap was taken from, and depending on how much tissue was needed, the position of the breast or nipple may be slightly altered. The scar typically fades and becomes less noticeable over time, and support with camouflage make-up is available if this is a concern for you.

Will my arm and shoulder work normally afterwards?

Most patients regain good use of the arm and shoulder. Movement is often uncomfortable in the first few days, so only gentle movement is recommended initially, with a structured physiotherapy exercise plan to follow to help you regain full movement and strength.

What happens if the flap develops a problem with its blood supply?

This affects around 2 to 3% of patients, usually becoming apparent within the first two days, and is treated as an emergency — meaning an urgent return to theatre to try to clear the affected blood vessel. This isn't always successful, and if the flap cannot be saved, your surgeon will use an alternative method of reconstruction instead.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, or any change in the colour or warmth of the reconstructed area or the flap. Use the contact details below.
