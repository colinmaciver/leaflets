Scar Revision

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains scar revision, which aims to improve or disguise an existing scar, so that you can talk it through with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team.

At a glance
  • Scar revision improves how a scar looks. It cannot remove a scar completely.
  • It is not normally done until at least six months after the original injury or operation, because scars improve on their own with time.
  • Depending on the scar it may mean cutting it out and re-stitching, laser or abrasion, or injections and dressings.
  • Looking after the wound and scar afterwards (moisturiser massage, sun protection, sometimes silicone) matters as much as the operation.

1. Why scar revision has been suggested

Facial scars can follow injury or surgery. Many fade to a fine pale line. Some stay wide, raised, thick, sunken, dark or red, or pull on the surrounding skin. Scar revision is designed to improve or disguise the appearance of a scar.

Scars improve on their own over many months, so surgery is not usually done for at least six months after the injury or operation. Your surgeon will examine your scar and talk to you about what is realistic. Even in the best hands, a scar can never be removed completely, and an unsightly scar can come back despite revision.

Non-surgical options

Depending on the scar, treatments that avoid a new operation may be suggested first or in addition: silicone gel or sheeting, steroid injections (for thick or raised scars), laser, massage and sun protection.

2. The anaesthetic

Scar revision can be done under local anaesthetic (an injection to make the area numb) or under general anaesthetic (fully asleep), depending on the size and site of the scar.

If you are having a general anaesthetic: before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic - this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. You will not be able to drive or operate machinery for 48 hours afterwards.

3. What the operation involves

There are many types of facial scar, and treatment depends on the type and size. Common methods include:

  • Cutting out and re-stitching: the scar is removed and the wound closed carefully in a better line, often following the natural skin creases.
  • Changing the direction of the scar (for example Z-plasty or W-plasty), so that it is broken up, lies along natural lines or no longer pulls on the skin.
  • Abrasion or laser surgery: the surface of the scar is smoothed with a laser or abrasion. These need no stitches.
  • Filling a sunken scar, for example with your own fat (see the fat grafting leaflet).
  • Steroid injections or other add-on treatments for thick, raised scars.

Stitches

Deep stitches are dissolvable. Skin stitches on a visible facial scar are often removed after about a week. Your surgeon will confirm which type of stitch is used. A dressing may be applied over the wound.

4. What to expect afterwards

  • Scar revision is not usually very painful. Simple painkillers such as paracetamol or ibuprofen (if safe for you) are usually enough. Discomfort is worse for the first few days and settles in about a week.
  • If your surgeon thinks infection might be a problem you will be given a course of antibiotics.
  • Swelling and bruising vary and are worst for the first few days. Cold compresses and sleeping propped upright help.
  • If the surgery is under local anaesthetic you go home straight away. After a general anaesthetic you may stay overnight.
  • Most people take a couple of days off work, depending on the job and the site.
  • A scar revision looks red for several weeks. It takes many months to reach its final appearance.

Caring for your wound

Keep the wound clean and dry until your surgeon tells you it is safe to wash. When washing is allowed, wash gently with water and pat dry; do not rub. Avoid soaking (baths, swimming) until it is healed. Follow any dressing instructions you are given. Depending on the scar, dressing may continue after the stitches are removed.

Caring for your scar once healed

  • Moisturise and massage: apply a plain moisturising cream with firm massage twice a day for two to five minutes. Continue for at least three months. Many scars keep improving for up to a year, so keep going for as long as it helps and as your surgeon advises.
  • Silicone gel or sheeting (optional): the best-supported product for scars that are becoming thick or raised. Your surgeon will tell you if it would help you.
  • Sun protection: a new scar burns and darkens more easily than normal skin, and this can become permanent. Use a strong sunscreen (SPF 30 to 50) whenever the scar is exposed to sunlight for the first 6 to 12 months.

5. Possible complications

  • Bleeding and swelling: some oozing is common in the first day. If it continues at home, press firmly with a clean handkerchief or swab for at least 10 minutes.
  • New wound and new scar: revision replaces one scar with another, hopefully a better one. It looks red for several weeks.
  • Infection: uncommon, especially if antibiotics have been given.
  • Delayed healing or the wound opening.
  • Raised, thick scar (hypertrophic scar or keloid): more likely in some people and some body sites. Silicone, injections or other treatments may be suggested.
  • Colour changes: the scar or skin around it may be darker or paler.
  • The scar coming back or no improvement: even in the best hands, an unsightly scar can reappear.
  • Dissatisfaction and need for further treatment.

No reliable percentage figures exist for scar revision, because results depend so much on the type of scar and your skin, so we use qualitative terms.

Contact the clinic or the Emergency Department if you have increasing pain, redness or swelling, discharge or an unpleasant smell, the wound opening, or a fever.

Questions patients often ask

Why do I have to wait six months?

Scars keep changing for many months and often improve by themselves. Operating too early can leave a worse scar.

Can the scar be removed completely?

No. The aim is a scar that is less noticeable, flatter, narrower or better placed.

Will the revised scar be better than the original?

Usually yes, but it cannot be promised. Your surgeon will explain what is realistic for your scar.

What can I do myself to help my scar?

Keep the wound clean and dry, massage with moisturiser, use silicone if advised and protect the scar from the sun.
