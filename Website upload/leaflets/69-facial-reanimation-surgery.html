Facial Reanimation Surgery

An overview of the surgical options for restoring movement and symmetry after facial nerve palsy

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has this been recommended?

Facial nerve palsy — weakness or paralysis of the muscles on one or both sides of the face — can happen for a number of reasons, including Bell's palsy, injury, or as part of treatment for a tumour affecting the facial nerve (for further background on the condition itself, see the separate Facial Nerve Palsy / Bell's Palsy leaflet). Depending on the cause and how long the palsy has been present, this can affect your ability to close your eye fully, to control your mouth and smile, and to show facial expression normally.

Facial reanimation surgery covers a range of different operations aimed at restoring movement, symmetry, and protective function (particularly eye closure) to a paralysed face. There is no single operation used for everyone — the right option depends on your individual circumstances, and this leaflet gives a brief overview of the main options so you understand what is being suggested for you and why. It is not a detailed description of any one technique; your surgeon will go through the specific operation recommended for you in more depth.

2. What are the treatment options?

The overall goal of treatment is to restore as much symmetry and movement as possible across the upper, middle, and lower parts of the face. Options are generally considered as a stepwise ladder, from simpler, less invasive procedures through to more complex microsurgical operations, and are often combined or staged rather than used in isolation. The main options, broadly in order of increasing complexity, are:

OptionWhat it involvesWhen it's typically considered
Static proceduresNon-dynamic techniques that support the paralysed side without restoring active movement — for example a small weight placed in the upper eyelid to help it close, or a sling of tissue used to support and lift the corner of the mouth.Long-standing palsy, patients unsuitable for longer microsurgical operations, or as a first step to protect the eye while other options are considered.
Cross-facial nerve grafting (CFNG)A nerve graft is used to connect a branch of the healthy facial nerve on the unaffected side across to the paralysed side, aiming to restore a spontaneous, natural smile over time.Within around 2 years of the onset of palsy, before the facial muscles have wasted away, often combined with a later free muscle transfer.
Nerve-to-masseter transferA nerve that normally supplies part of the chewing muscle (masseter) is redirected to reanimate the facial muscles instead.Can give a relatively faster return of movement than some other nerve options, and is sometimes used after an earlier reanimation attempt has not fully succeeded.
Hypoglossal nerve transferPart of the nerve that normally supplies the tongue is redirected to the facial nerve to restore muscle tone and movement.Used in selected cases, particularly where other nerve options are not suitable; modern techniques use only part of the nerve to reduce the effect on tongue function.
Temporalis muscle/tendon transferA chewing muscle at the temple (temporalis) is repositioned, or its tendon rerouted, and attached near the corner of the mouth to recreate a smiling movement using a bite-down action.Useful where a working nerve for a free muscle transfer is not available or suitable, or as an additional procedure alongside other reanimation techniques.
Gracilis free flapA small muscle from the inner thigh (gracilis) is transferred to the face as a free flap, with its blood vessels reconnected under surgical loupe magnification and its nerve connected to a suitable donor nerve (such as the masseter nerve or a cross-facial nerve graft) to bring the new muscle to life.Longer-standing or complete facial paralysis where a new, actively contracting muscle is needed to recreate movement, most often for the smile.
TUG (transverse upper gracilis) flapA variant of the gracilis free flap that includes a paddle of skin from the inner thigh along with the muscle, again reconnected under surgical loupe magnification.Considered when the surgical team also needs a skin paddle — for example to help monitor the flap's blood supply after surgery, or to replace missing skin as well as muscle.

Several of these options are often combined, or performed in stages months apart (for example, a cross-facial nerve graft followed later by a free muscle transfer once the graft has grown across), rather than as a single one-off procedure.

3. How is the right option chosen for me?

  • How long you have had facial palsy — nerve-based options generally work best within around 2 years of onset, before the facial muscles have wasted away; beyond this, a free muscle transfer or static procedure is more likely to be recommended.
  • Which part(s) of your face are affected — the upper face (eye closure and brow), middle face (cheek and nose), and lower face (mouth and smile) can each need a different approach, and more than one procedure may be needed to address all of them.
  • Your general fitness and medical history — some options, particularly free flap surgery, involve a longer operation and general anaesthetic, so your fitness for this will be assessed carefully at an anaesthetic assessment before your operation.
  • Your own goals and priorities — for example, whether protecting your eye, restoring symmetry at rest, or recreating a voluntary smile matters most to you, which your surgeon will discuss with you in detail.
  • Whether you have had previous facial reanimation surgery — for example, a nerve-to-masseter transfer or a further free muscle transfer may be recommended as a next step if an earlier attempt has not fully achieved the desired result.

4. What outcome can I expect?

The simpler static procedures (such as an eyelid weight) usually have an immediate, visible effect, since they do not rely on nerve regrowth. Nerve-based and muscle transfer options work differently: it typically takes several months, and sometimes over a year, for a transferred nerve or muscle to begin producing visible movement, because the nerve fibres need time to grow into their new target. Improvement then tends to continue gradually over the following months as the new nerve connections mature.

It is important to have realistic expectations: the goal of facial reanimation surgery is meaningful improvement in symmetry, protection (particularly of the eye), and function, and — for many of the more complex options — some return of voluntary or even spontaneous movement. However, most operations do not fully restore the face to exactly how it was before the palsy, and further, staged procedures are sometimes needed to build on an earlier result. Your surgeon will discuss what is realistic for your specific situation and chosen option before you decide to proceed.

5. Possible risks and complications

The specific risks depend on which option is recommended for you, and your surgeon will go through these in detail for your particular procedure. General risks that apply across most of these operations include:

  • Risks of a general anaesthetic, for options performed under one — before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic, which is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.
  • Bleeding, infection, and scarring at the operated site, and at any donor site (such as the inner thigh for a gracilis or TUG flap).
  • The transferred nerve or muscle not working as well as hoped, or not producing the degree of movement expected, which occasionally means a further procedure is needed.
  • For free flap options (gracilis and TUG flap), a small risk that the transferred tissue does not survive due to a problem with its blood supply, which would need an urgent return to theatre or use of an alternative option.
  • Donor site effects specific to the nerve or muscle used — for example, some patients notice mild, usually temporary, tongue weakness after a hypoglossal nerve transfer, or altered sensation and a scar at the inner thigh after a gracilis or TUG flap.
  • Overcorrection, undercorrection, or asymmetry that needs a further adjustment procedure to fine-tune the result.

Questions patients often ask

Will I need more than one operation?

Possibly. Several facial reanimation techniques are performed in stages months apart (for example, a cross-facial nerve graft followed later by a free muscle transfer), and some patients benefit from a further adjustment procedure once the initial result has settled.

How long before I see an improvement?

Static procedures usually have an immediate effect. Nerve-based and muscle transfer options typically take several months, and sometimes over a year, before movement becomes visible, with gradual improvement continuing after that.

Will my face look completely normal afterwards?

Most operations aim for meaningful improvement in symmetry, protection, and function rather than a perfect return to how your face looked before the palsy. Your surgeon will discuss realistic expectations for your specific situation.

Is this always done under general anaesthetic?

No — this depends on the option. Some static procedures can be done under local anaesthetic or with sedation, while nerve transfer and free flap operations are performed under general anaesthetic. Your surgeon will confirm this for your specific procedure.

What if a previous facial reanimation procedure hasn't worked as well as hoped?

Further options are usually available, such as a nerve-to-masseter transfer or a free muscle transfer, which can be considered as a next step. Your surgeon will assess your individual situation to advise on the best way forward.
