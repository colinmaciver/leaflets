Having an MRI Scan

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains what to expect when your surgeon and OMFS clinic team arrange an MRI scan to help understand your condition.

At a glance
  • An MRI scan uses strong magnets and radio waves, not X-rays.
  • The scan is painless but it is noisy and you must keep still.
  • You will fill in a safety form because the magnet can affect some metal implants.
  • It usually takes around 30 minutes for one body area, sometimes longer.
  • You can usually eat, drink and take your medicines as normal.
  • The report takes several days, and your results will be discussed at a clinic appointment.

1. What is an MRI scan and why is it done?

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) builds up a detailed picture of your body and its organs. Unlike a CT scan, it uses strong magnetic fields and radio waves rather than X-rays.

MRI is especially good for showing soft tissues such as the tongue, glands, muscles, nerves, the jaw joint and lumps in the head and neck. Your surgeon may ask for it to find the cause of your symptoms, to plan treatment or to check progress.

2. Is it safe?

There are no known harmful effects from the MRI scan itself. However, because the magnet is very strong, MRI cannot be done, or needs special care, in people with certain metal implants. These include some pacemakers, inner ear (cochlear) implants, some surgical clips in the head and some metallic heart valves.

Please tell the team about any implants, clips, plates, screws or previous operations, as well as about any metal fragments in the eyes, for example from welding or an injury. You may need an X-ray of your eyes first to make sure no fragments remain. Tell us too if you are or might be pregnant.

You will be asked to remove all metal items, including watch, jewellery, hearing aids and bank cards, and may be given a gown. Some dental work or make-up can affect the pictures, so please let the team know.

Noise

The magnets make loud banging, buzzing and rumbling noises. You will be given headphones or earplugs to reduce the noise.

3. What does the scan involve?

You lie on a couch that slides into a tunnel-shaped scanner. You will have a call button in your hand and the radiographer can hear and talk to you throughout. For head and neck scans, a frame or coil is placed around your head or neck.

Will I need an injection?

Some patients need an injection of dye (contrast) to improve the quality of the scan. In most people it is given in a vein in the arm or elbow, in the same way as a blood test. The injection should have no after-effects. Tell us if you have kidney problems.

How long will it take?

It usually takes around 30 minutes to scan one part of the body. It takes a little longer to scan more than one area. A longer scan does not mean that something is wrong.

Does it hurt?

No. The scan is painless but you must keep very still and lie quite flat. The scanner is like a tunnel and some people feel claustrophobic. If you are worried, tell the team beforehand, as they can help.

4. Before and after

You can usually eat, drink and take your usual medicines before your MRI scan. If you are having a scan of your tummy you may be asked not to eat for about four hours beforehand. Afterwards you can go home and carry on as normal.

5. Results

It takes a long time to study all the pictures, so the report will not be available for several days. You will be sent an appointment to see your surgeon in the outpatient clinic to discuss the findings and any further treatment that might be needed.

A scan cannot always give a complete answer, and sometimes further tests are needed. Your surgeon will explain what the scan shows and how this fits with the plan for your care.

Questions patients often ask

I have a pacemaker or other implant. Can I still have an MRI?

Sometimes yes, with special checks. Tell us as soon as you know you are booked so the team can plan.

Can I bring someone with me?

Yes, but they usually cannot come into the scan room because of the magnet.

Can I drive afterwards?

Yes, in most cases.
