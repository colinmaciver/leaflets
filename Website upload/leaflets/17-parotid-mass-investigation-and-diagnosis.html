A Lump in the Parotid Gland: Tests and Diagnosis

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains the tests used to investigate a lump in the parotid gland, what the lump might be, and how the results guide treatment. It is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team.

At a glance
  • The parotid glands lie in front of the ears. A lump here is common and most are benign (not cancer).
  • Tests usually include an ultrasound scan, often an MRI or CT scan, and a needle test.
  • Scans and needle tests give valuable information but cannot always give a final answer.
  • The only way to be certain what a lump is, is to remove it and examine it under the microscope.
  • Your case will be discussed by a specialist team before treatment is agreed with you.

1. What is a parotid lump?

You have two parotid glands, one in front of each ear. They make saliva. A lump develops when a group of cells grows abnormally. The exact reasons are usually not clear.

Most parotid lumps (around 80%) are benign. This means they are not cancer and do not spread to other parts of the body. Occasionally, a lump is cancerous. Lumps can also be a lymph node, a cyst or an inflamed area.

The facial nerve, which moves the muscles of the face, runs through the parotid gland. This is important when planning any treatment.

2. How is a parotid lump assessed?

Your surgeon will ask how long the lump has been there, whether it has grown, and whether you have pain, numbness or weakness of the face. They will examine your face, mouth and neck, check how well your facial muscles move, and feel the lymph nodes in the neck. Your skin and scalp may also be checked, because some skin cancers can spread to lymph nodes in the parotid.

Ultrasound scan

A painless scan using sound waves. It shows the size and shape of the lump and whether it looks solid or fluid-filled. It also looks at the lymph nodes in the neck, and is used to guide a needle.

MRI and CT scans

An MRI scan uses magnets to show the lump in detail, including how close it is to the facial nerve and nearby structures. A CT scan uses X-rays and can be used to see surrounding bone and the neck. Sometimes a PET scan is used to look at the whole body. You will be told how to prepare. Please tell the team if you have a pacemaker or metal implants, or if you are or may be pregnant.

Needle tests

  • Fine needle aspiration (FNA): a thin needle takes a small sample of cells. It is done at the same time as the ultrasound and may be uncomfortable for a moment.
  • Core biopsy: a slightly larger needle takes a thin sample of tissue. It gives more information, but is more uncomfortable, so it is only used when needed. Local anaesthetic is used.

The samples are examined under the microscope by a specialist pathologist. Results usually take several days, and you will be given an appointment to discuss them.

3. What could the lump be?

  • Pleomorphic adenoma: the most common parotid tumour. It is benign, grows slowly, and does not go away. Over many years a small number can become cancerous, which is one reason removal is advised.
  • Warthin's tumour: a benign, slow-growing lump, more common in older people and in smokers.
  • Other benign lumps: cysts, lymph nodes and less common benign growths.
  • Salivary gland cancer: there are several types, which behave differently. Some grow slowly and others more quickly.
  • Spread from a skin cancer: skin cancers of the face and scalp can spread to lymph nodes in the parotid.
  • Lymphoma: a cancer of the lymph system. It is treated differently from other lumps.

Warning signs that make us more concerned include a fast-growing lump, pain, numbness, a hard or fixed lump, or any weakness of the face. Please tell your team if you notice any of these.

4. What do the results mean for treatment?

  • Benign lump: surgery to remove the lump along with a margin of normal gland is usually recommended. This is called a parotidectomy. Simply scooping out a lump is not advised, because it can leave cells behind and the lump can return.
  • Cancer or suspected cancer: surgery is often the main treatment. Sometimes lymph nodes in the neck are removed, and further treatment such as radiotherapy may be advised.
  • Lymphoma: treatment is usually medical rather than surgical.
  • An unclear result: removal is often still the best way to find out what the lump is.

A parotidectomy is done under general anaesthetic through a cut in front of the ear that curves behind it. The facial nerve is identified and protected, and a nerve monitor may be used. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

Occasionally a lump turns out to be a cancer once it has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

The MDT includes head and neck surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, oncologists, specialist nurses, speech and swallowing therapists and dietitians. It exists to make sure that you get the best and safest plan.

5. Risks of parotid surgery

  • Weakness of the face: temporary weakness occurs in about 15 to 20 in 100 people (15-20%), more often where the lump is very close to the nerve. It usually recovers in a few weeks. Permanent weakness after surgery for benign lumps is rare, at around 1 in 100.
  • Numbness of the ear lobe and nearby skin: common, usually improving, though the ear lobe may stay numb.
  • Blood clot (haematoma): about 1 in 20 people (around 5%). Occasionally a return to theatre is needed.
  • Saliva collection (fistula): around 1 in 40 (about 2.5%). It can be drawn off with a needle.
  • Frey's syndrome: sweating and redness of the cheek when eating, which usually responds to a roll-on antiperspirant.

A small drain is usually placed for 24 to 48 hours and stitches are dissolvable. Most people are home after one or two nights. Your surgeon will explain what applies to you.

6. Why is removal recommended even when tests suggest a benign lump?

Scans and needle tests are very useful, but they only show part of the picture. A needle takes a tiny sample, and a scan shows shape and position, not the cells themselves. Because of this, there is always some uncertainty about the exact nature of a lump until it has been removed and examined fully.

This is why surgery is often recommended. Removing the lump gives you a definite answer, stops it growing larger (which makes surgery harder and the scar bigger), and removes the small chance that a benign lump changes over time. It also lets the team plan any further care properly. Our aim is to give you the clearest answer and the best long-term outcome, with the facial nerve protected.

Questions patients often ask

Does having a needle test spread a tumour?

No. Needle tests are safe and widely used. The risk of spreading cells along the needle path is extremely small.

Can I just watch the lump?

In some cases, such as a very small benign-looking lump, or if you are not fit for surgery, this can be discussed. Most lumps continue to grow, so removal is usually advised.

How long until I get my results?

Needle test results usually take several days. Final results from the removed lump take 1 to 2 weeks. Your team will arrange a follow-up appointment.

Can I get a second opinion?

Yes. Signing a consent form does not mean you must go ahead, and your consultant will gladly help you arrange a second opinion.
