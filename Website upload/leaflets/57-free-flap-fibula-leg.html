Free Flap Reconstruction — Fibula (Leg)

Using bone from your lower leg, with its own blood supply, to rebuild your jaw

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has this been recommended?

When a substantial part of your jawbone is removed during head and neck surgery — most often as part of surgery for a jaw or mouth cancer — reconstruction is needed to rebuild it and restore your ability to chew, speak, and support your facial shape. The fibula, one of the two bones in the lower leg, is the most commonly used source of bone for this: it provides a long, strong length of bone with its own reliable blood supply, which can be carefully cut and shaped to recreate the curve of the jaw, and can carry a paddle of overlying skin as well if soft tissue is also needed.

This is 'free' tissue transfer (a free flap), meaning the bone and tissue are completely disconnected from the leg, moved to the jaw, and its blood vessels are then rejoined under magnification to vessels in the neck. Before your operation, the blood supply to your leg and foot is checked (usually with a scan of the leg vessels) to confirm the fibula can be safely used without affecting the circulation to your foot. A second surgical team usually raises the flap from your leg at the same time as your jaw is being prepared elsewhere, so the two parts of the operation often run in parallel to keep total surgery time as short as possible.

2. What does the anaesthetic involve?

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable. Combining jaw reconstruction with resection of the original problem is major surgery and commonly takes several hours, and most patients spend time in a high-dependency or intensive care unit afterwards for close monitoring.

3. What does the procedure involve?

  • An incision is made along the outer side of the lower leg, and the muscles are carefully separated from the fibula bone.
  • If a skin paddle is needed as well as bone, this is raised together with the fibula, based on the same blood vessels.
  • The fibula and its blood vessels (the peroneal artery and vein) are freed along their length, and the bone is cut to the length needed and removed with its blood supply intact.
  • The flap is moved to the jaw, where the bone is carefully shaped — sometimes in more than one segment — to recreate the missing section, and secured with titanium plates and screws.
  • Under surgical loupe magnification, the flap's own artery and vein are carefully joined to blood vessels in your neck, restoring its blood supply in its new position.
  • Once connected, the flap's blood flow is monitored very closely, usually with checks every hour for the first day or two, since a healthy blood supply is essential for it to survive.
  • The leg donor site is closed in layers; if a skin paddle was taken and the defect is too wide to close directly, a split-thickness skin graft (see the separate Skin Grafting leaflet) is used to cover it, and a drain is usually left in the leg for a few days.

4. What is the expected outcome?

Immediately afterwards

  • Most patients spend the first one to two days in a high-dependency or intensive care unit for close monitoring of the flap's blood supply.
  • Your leg will be bandaged, elevated to reduce swelling, and a drain may be in place for a few days.
  • You will need support from the physiotherapy team to begin mobilising, and will usually be advised to keep weight off the operated leg to some degree at first, gradually building up as healing allows. A typical pattern is bed rest for the first day or two, then sitting out in a chair, with walking starting towards the end of the first week.

As you recover

  • Swelling in the lower leg and ankle is common in the first few weeks and can take longer to fully settle than at other donor sites, particularly with prolonged standing — elevating the leg when resting helps.
  • Walking gradually improves as swelling settles and strength returns; most patients regain a normal walking pattern, though this can take some months, especially if a skin graft was needed at the donor site. A typical pattern is near-normal walking and climbing stairs by around the end of the second week, occasionally with the help of a stick for a further week or so.
  • The donor site scar, and the reconstructed jaw, continue to heal and settle over several months.
  • Your progress is followed up regularly, and if this reconstruction was combined with cancer surgery, a follow-up appointment is arranged to discuss the pathology results and any further treatment needed.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications are possible, but the great majority of patients recover well from this operation, and your team monitors closely for these and has clear plans to manage them. The approximate figure below is drawn from Mr Maciver's own surgical experience:

  • Pain, swelling, bleeding, and infection — expected to some degree at both the jaw and the leg donor site, and settling with simple measures if they occur.
  • Flap failure — the flap loses part or all of its blood supply and does not survive, occurring in approximately 4% of cases based on Mr Maciver's own outcomes data. This most often becomes apparent in the first few days, which is why monitoring is so frequent, and may require an urgent return to theatre or, occasionally, an alternative form of reconstruction.
  • A collection of blood or fluid under the flap or at the donor site (a haematoma or seroma), which may need drainage.
  • Donor site problems, including prolonged leg or ankle swelling, delayed wound healing (particularly where a skin graft was used), altered sensation over part of the lower leg or foot, and, uncommonly, weakness affecting movement of the ankle or toes.
  • General risks of a longer operation and anaesthetic, including chest infection and blood clots in the leg (DVT) or lung (pulmonary embolism), which your anaesthetic assessment will take into account.
  • If this reconstruction was combined with cancer surgery, occasionally the final pathology is different or more extensive than expected once the tissue has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.
  • Further surgery may occasionally be needed to manage a complication or adjust the reconstructed jaw, for example before fitting dental implants.
Your surgical team monitors closely for these complications and has clear plans in place to manage them if they occur. Please raise any worries with your team — they are used to these questions and would rather you ask.

Questions patients often ask

Why is bone being taken from my leg?

The fibula provides a long, strong piece of bone with a very reliable blood supply, which can be shaped to closely recreate the missing part of your jaw. It is the most commonly used bone source for jaw reconstruction. Your surgeon will explain why this donor site has been chosen for your reconstruction.

Will removing part of my fibula affect how my leg works?

The fibula is a supporting bone rather than the main weight-bearing bone of the lower leg, so most patients do not notice a difference in leg strength or stability once healing is complete. Before surgery, your leg's blood supply is checked to confirm it is safe to use this bone.

Will I be able to walk normally afterwards?

Most patients return to a normal walking pattern, though recovery can take longer than after some other donor sites, particularly if the wound needed a skin graft. A typical pattern is bed rest for the first day or two, walking from around the end of the first week, and near-normal walking and stairs by around the end of the second week — occasionally with the help of a stick for a further week or so. Your physiotherapy team will guide your mobilisation and weight-bearing as you heal.

Can I have dental implants in the reconstructed jaw later?

Often, yes — fibula bone can support dental implants once healing is complete, though this depends on your individual reconstruction and future dental assessment.

What happens if the flap fails?

This is uncommon (around 4% of cases based on Mr Maciver's own outcomes data) but is treated as an emergency if it happens, usually meaning an urgent return to theatre to try to restore blood flow, or occasionally a different form of reconstruction. Your team monitors closely specifically to catch this early.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, or any change in the colour or warmth of the reconstructed area or your leg. Use the contact details below.
