Head and Neck Cancer: Early Signs and Reducing Your Risk

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains the early warning signs of head and neck cancer and what you can do to lower your risk, so that problems can be seen by your surgeon and OMFS clinic team as early as possible.

At a glance
  • Head and neck cancer is much easier to treat when it is found early.
  • If you have any ONE of the warning signs for THREE weeks, see your doctor or dentist.
  • Smoking and tobacco are the biggest risk factors. Alcohol adds to the risk, and some throat cancers are linked to the HPV virus.
  • Stopping smoking and tobacco at any age lowers your risk.
  • Most people with these symptoms do not have cancer, but they must be checked.

1. What is head and neck cancer?

Head and neck cancer is the name for cancers that begin in the mouth, throat, voice box (larynx), nose and sinuses, and salivary glands. It does not include cancers of the brain or eye. Together these account for roughly 5 in 100 cancers worldwide. More than 9 in 10 begin in the flat (squamous) cells that line the inside of the mouth, nose and throat.

It is more common in people over 40 and in men, but it is becoming more common in women and in younger people, partly because of HPV-related throat cancer.

In many countries about 2 in 3 of these cancers are found at an advanced stage, when they are harder to treat. Of those, about half are not alive 5 years later. When the cancer is found and treated early, around 80 to 90 in 100 people survive. This is why early diagnosis matters.

2. Warning signs: the 'One for Three' rule

See your GP, family doctor or dentist if you have ANY ONE of these for more than THREE weeks:

  • a sore tongue, a mouth ulcer that does not heal, or a red or white patch in the mouth
  • pain in the throat
  • a lump in the neck
  • pain or difficulty when swallowing
  • a hoarse voice that does not get better
  • a blocked nose on one side, and/or bloody discharge from the nose

Other things worth reporting include loose teeth with no clear cause, numbness of the lip or tongue, pain in one ear without ear disease, and unexplained weight loss.

Most people who have one of these symptoms do not have cancer. It is usually something simple, such as an ulcer or an infection. But if it lasts three weeks, please have it checked. Early referral and early diagnosis save lives.

3. What increases your risk?

  • Smoking. Current smokers are about 4 to 5 times more likely to develop mouth, throat (oropharynx and hypopharynx) cancer, and about 10 times more likely to develop cancer of the voice box, than people who have never smoked. In the UK, smoking is thought to cause about 2 in 10 mouth cancers, 4 in 10 throat cancers and 6 in 10 voice box cancers.
  • Other tobacco and chewing products, such as chewing tobacco, paan, betel quid (areca nut) and shisha or water pipes.
  • Alcohol. Drinking more than about 3 units a day for men, or 2 for women, raises the risk significantly: about 5 times the risk of mouth and throat cancer at 6 or more units a day. Alcohol and tobacco together raise the risk further than either alone, to roughly 5 to 14 times the risk of people who do neither if both are heavy.
  • HPV (human papillomavirus). Certain types of HPV cause a rising number of throat cancers, particularly at the back of the tongue and in the tonsils.
  • Sun. Lip cancer is linked to strong sun exposure.
  • Poor diet, poor mouth care and long-standing mouth patches (such as red or white patches that change) can also contribute.

4. How can you lower your risk?

  • Stop smoking and all forms of tobacco. Your doctor or pharmacist can help with support and medicines. Your risk starts to fall after you stop and keeps falling over time.
  • Keep alcohol low. UK guidance is to drink no more than 14 units a week, spread over several days.
  • Eat plenty of fruit and vegetables.
  • Protect your lips with a lip balm containing sunscreen and wear a hat in strong sun.
  • Brush twice a day, see your dentist regularly and have ill-fitting dentures adjusted.
  • Ask about HPV vaccination, which helps prevent the infections linked to some throat cancers.
  • Look in your mouth every month and feel your neck for lumps. Know what is normal for you.

5. What happens if I am referred?

You will usually be seen by a head and neck surgeon in an OMFS clinic. The doctor will examine your mouth, throat and neck, and may use a thin flexible camera to look at your throat. You may have a biopsy (small sample), scans or an ultrasound. If a cancer is found, your case is reviewed by the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT), where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this is explained to you in person. Many conditions that are found are not cancer, and are simply treated.

Questions patients often ask

I have had a mouth ulcer for two weeks. Should I worry?

Most ulcers heal within two weeks. If it is still there at three weeks, see your doctor or dentist.

I stopped smoking years ago. Am I still at risk?

Your risk falls with time after stopping, so you are better off. Still report any symptoms.

Can I get head and neck cancer without smoking or drinking?

Yes. Some cancers, such as HPV-related throat cancer, occur in non-smokers. Any lasting symptom should be checked.

Who should I see first?

Your GP, family doctor or dentist. They will arrange a referral if it is needed.
