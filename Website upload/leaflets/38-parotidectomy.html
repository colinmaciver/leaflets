Parotidectomy

Surgery to remove part or all of the parotid (salivary) gland

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

At a glance

  • What happens during the operation, including the anaesthetic

1. Why has surgery been recommended?

Your parotid glands are the largest of your salivary glands. You have one on each side of your face, sitting just in front of and below your ear, and extending down towards the angle of your jaw. They produce saliva that drains into your mouth to help with chewing, swallowing and digestion.

Parotidectomy — surgery to remove part or all of the gland — is usually recommended when a lump or swelling has developed within it, in order to remove it and find out exactly what it is. It is occasionally also needed for a gland that keeps becoming infected, or that contains a stone blocking its duct.

Most parotid lumps turn out to be benign (non-cancerous) growths such as a pleomorphic adenoma or Warthin's tumour. However, it can be difficult to be completely certain what a lump is from scans or a needle sample alone, and some benign lumps can grow larger over time or, rarely, undergo change into a cancer if left untreated. This uncertainty is exactly why surgical removal is usually recommended: taking out the whole lump allows a specialist pathologist to examine it fully and tell you precisely what it is, giving you and your surgical team a clear, reliable diagnosis on which to base any further care.

2. The anaesthetic

Parotidectomy is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout and will be aware of nothing until the operation is complete. It typically takes two to four hours, depending on the size of the gland involved and the complexity of the lump. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable.

3. What does the procedure involve?

The facial nerve

Running through the middle of the parotid gland, dividing it into an outer (superficial) part and a deeper part, is the facial nerve. This is the nerve that controls all the movements on that side of your face — your smile, closing your eye, and raising your eyebrow. Protecting this nerve is the single most important part of a parotidectomy.

A nerve monitor and surgical magnification (loupes) are used routinely throughout the operation. Together, these let the surgical team see the nerve and its branches more clearly and confirm they are working correctly at each stage, reducing the risk of injury to the nerve as the gland is removed from around it.

The procedure

  • An incision is made just in front of your ear, curving down behind the earlobe and into a natural crease in your upper neck — the same well-established line used for a facelift incision — to keep the resulting scar as inconspicuous as possible and give a good aesthetic result.
  • The surgeon dissects down to the gland and identifies the facial nerve where it emerges from the skull, then carefully traces its branches using magnification, with the nerve monitor confirming the nerve is working throughout.
  • Depending on the size and position of the lump, either the outer part of the gland only (a superficial parotidectomy) or the whole gland (a total parotidectomy) is removed, working around each nerve branch to keep it intact unless it is directly involved by a cancer.
  • The lump and surrounding gland tissue is sent to a specialist pathologist, who examines it under the microscope to confirm exactly what it is. This usually takes one to two weeks and is explained to you at a follow-up appointment.
  • A head bandage is applied at the end of the operation to compress the wound for 24 to 48 hours and reduce the risk of fluid collecting underneath. A drain is not routinely used for this operation, though one may occasionally be placed instead if a compression bandage is not suitable in your case.

4. What to expect afterwards

Immediately afterwards

  • Most patients stay in hospital as a day case or for one night, though this varies depending on your operation and general health.
  • If a drain has been used instead of a bandage, it is usually removed after one to two days once drainage has settled.
  • The stitches used to close the wound are all dissolvable, so there is nothing that needs to be removed — they disappear on their own over the following weeks.
  • The head bandage is usually kept in place for the first 48 hours and can then be removed at home.
  • You will be given antibiotics for the day of, and sometimes the day after, surgery, and regular pain relief.

As you recover

  • Swelling and bruising around the wound and jaw are normal for the first one to three weeks.
  • Most people manage light day-to-day activities within one to two weeks, with fuller activity, including a return to work, typically by three to four weeks depending on how physical your role is.
  • Numbness of the ear lobe and any facial nerve weakness generally continue to improve over several months, and the great majority of patients regain full or near-full facial movement.
  • A follow-up appointment is arranged to give you the pathology results and discuss whether any further treatment is needed.
  • If the final pathology shows a cancer, your case will be discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, and the recommended plan will then be explained to you in person at your follow-up appointment.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications of parotidectomy are possible, but the great majority of patients recover without experiencing any of them, and most of those that do occur are minor and settle by themselves. The approximate figures below are taken from published studies of large numbers of patients, so you can make an informed decision — your own surgeon will discuss which are most relevant to your individual case:

  • Bleeding, bruising or a collection of blood under the skin (a haematoma) — occurs in around 1 in 30 operations, occasionally needing a short return to theatre to drain it.
  • An unsightly scar — uncommon, since the incision follows the same line used for a facelift and usually heals inconspicuously.
  • Sensory nerve changes — numbness of the ear lobe and surrounding skin is very common, affecting around 1 in 3 patients to some degree, and usually improves over several months, though a small permanent patch of numbness is possible.
  • Facial nerve weakness — a temporary weakness of part or all of the face on the operated side is reported in around 1 in 5 to 1 in 3 patients in published studies, usually recovering over weeks to a few months as the nerve settles after being handled during surgery. Permanent weakness is much less common, reported in around 1 in 20 to 1 in 30 patients, and is more likely if the tumour is directly attached to, or growing into, the nerve.
  • Frey's syndrome (gustatory sweating) — unusual sweating and flushing of the skin over the cheek when eating, which can develop months after surgery as the nerves supplying sweat glands and saliva glands cross-regenerate. Noticeable symptoms affect roughly 1 in 3 patients, usually mild, and only occasionally troublesome enough to need further treatment.
  • First bite syndrome — a sharp, cramping pain in the area in front of the ear with the first bite of a meal, which then eases with further bites. This affects around 1 in 50 patients overall, rising to roughly 1 in 8 when surgery involves the deeper part of the gland, and it often improves over the following months.
  • A temporary collection of saliva under the skin (a sialocele) or a small leak from the wound (a salivary fistula) — occurs in around 1 in 20 patients and usually settles with simple pressure dressings and time.
  • A visible dent or hollow in the cheek where part of the gland has been removed — this is common, particularly after larger or deeper resections, and can be more noticeable in slimmer patients. It often becomes less obvious as swelling settles and the surrounding tissues adapt, though some patients are left with a permanent, mild contour change.
  • Wound infection — occurs in around 1 in 40 patients and usually settles with a course of antibiotics.
  • Different pathology than expected on final examination — occasionally a lump turns out to be a cancer once it has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.
Your surgical team monitors closely for these complications and has clear plans in place to manage them if they occur. Please raise any worries with your team — they are used to these questions and would rather you ask.

Questions patients often ask

Will I definitely lose movement in my face?

No. The facial nerve is carefully identified and protected throughout the operation, including with nerve monitoring, and most patients have completely normal facial movement afterwards. A temporary weakness, usually recovering over weeks to a few months, is reported in around 1 in 5 to 1 in 3 patients in published studies; permanent weakness is much less common, reported in around 1 in 20 to 1 in 30, and is discussed specifically with you if your surgeon feels there is a higher-than-usual risk in your case.

Why are a nerve monitor and magnification used during the operation?

They allow the surgical team to see the facial nerve and its branches more clearly and check it is working properly at each stage of the operation, which helps reduce the risk of injury to it as the gland is removed from around them.

What is Frey's syndrome, and can it be treated?

It's an unusual sweating or flushing of the cheek skin when eating, which can develop months after surgery, affecting roughly 1 in 3 patients to some degree. It's usually mild. If it becomes bothersome, treatments such as an antiperspirant applied to the area, or occasionally Botox injections, can help.

What is first bite syndrome?

A sharp cramping pain around the area of surgery with the very first bite of a meal, easing with further bites. It affects around 1 in 50 patients overall, rising to roughly 1 in 8 after surgery involving the deeper part of the gland, and tends to settle gradually over the following months.

Will my face look different afterwards?

Some patients notice a mild dent or hollow where part of the gland has been removed, particularly if a larger amount of tissue needed to be taken out. This is often most noticeable in the first few months while swelling settles, and tends to become less obvious as the surrounding tissues adapt, though a permanent mild contour change is possible. Your surgeon can discuss what to expect for your specific operation.

Will I need radiotherapy or further treatment?

This depends entirely on what the final pathology shows. Most parotid lumps are benign and need no further treatment beyond the operation. If a cancer is found, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting — a group of specialists who together agree the most appropriate plan — and this will then be explained to you in person by your surgeon before anything further is arranged.

How long will I be in hospital?

Most patients go home the same day or after one night, though this can vary depending on the extent of your surgery and your general health.

Will I have a visible scar?

The incision follows the same well-established line used for a facelift — just in front of the ear and into a natural neck crease — specifically to keep it as inconspicuous as possible, and it typically fades well over the following months to give a good aesthetic result.

When can I go back to work and normal activities?

Many patients manage light activities within one to two weeks and fuller activity, including work, within three to four weeks, though this varies with the extent of surgery and the physical demands of your role.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, or sudden new facial weakness. Use the contact details below.
