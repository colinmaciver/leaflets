Salivary Gland Swelling and Stones (Sialolithiasis)

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains why a salivary gland can swell, how stones form, and how they are investigated and treated. It is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team.

At a glance
  • Salivary stones are small hard deposits that can block the duct of a salivary gland.
  • Typical sign: pain and swelling under the jaw or in the cheek when you eat, which settles afterwards.
  • Most stones form in the submandibular gland, under the lower jaw.
  • Many stones can be removed with a tiny telescope (sialendoscopy), usually under local anaesthetic, without removing the gland.
  • Removing the gland is a last option when stones keep coming back.

1. What are salivary stones and why do they form?

Saliva drains from your glands into your mouth through small tubes called ducts. The submandibular duct opens under the tongue, near the front lower teeth. The parotid duct opens on the inside of the cheek, next to the upper back teeth.

Salivary stones are formed when minerals in the saliva clump together. They can range from the size of a grain of sand to a pea or larger. Most form in the submandibular gland, because its saliva is thicker and has to flow uphill. Fewer form in the parotid gland, and they are rare in the sublingual gland.

We do not always know why a stone forms. Dehydration, a dry mouth, some medicines, and previous infection or inflammation of the gland may play a part. Stones are not caused by diet and are not related to kidney stones.

Symptoms

  • Painful swelling of the gland, often when you see, smell or start eating food.
  • The swelling goes down over an hour or so, or longer.
  • A bad taste, or salty-tasting saliva.
  • A hard lump that you or your dentist can feel under the tongue or in the cheek.
  • If infection develops: constant pain, redness, fever and pus in the mouth.

A severe or long-standing blockage can lead to ongoing inflammation of the gland, which may become permanently swollen.

2. How is it assessed?

Your surgeon will examine your mouth and feel the gland and duct. Sometimes a stone can be felt directly. These tests may be arranged.

  • Ultrasound scan: painless, uses sound waves, and can show stones and a widened duct.
  • X-ray or CT scan: shows stones that contain a lot of calcium. Some stones, especially in the parotid, do not show on a plain X-ray.
  • Sialogram: the radiologist (X-ray doctor) passes a small blunt plastic tube into the duct and injects dye. X-ray pictures are then taken. It is not particularly painful and does not need a local anaesthetic injection. You may eat, drink and take your medicines beforehand. It takes about 30 minutes from start to finish, and a longer test does not mean something is wrong. You may feel some discomfort as the dye goes in. The report takes several days, and you will be sent an appointment to discuss the findings.
  • Sialendoscopy: a tiny telescope is used to look inside the duct. It can be a test and a treatment at the same time.

A sialogram is usually avoided if the gland is infected.

3. Self-care and conservative treatment

  • Drink plenty of water throughout the day.
  • Apply a warm compress and massage the gland gently towards the mouth.
  • Suck sugar-free or lemon-flavoured sweets to help saliva flow, once any infection has settled. Some small stones pass this way.
  • Keep your mouth clean and brush your teeth twice a day.
  • Take paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain, if suitable for you.
  • If the gland is infected, you will be given antibiotics. Take the whole course.
Seek urgent advice if you have a high fever, swelling that spreads into the neck, or difficulty swallowing, breathing or opening your mouth. Call SSMC on 800 7762, or attend the SSMC Emergency Department.

4. Treatment if the stone does not pass

Sialendoscopy

A miniature telescope (micro-endoscope) is passed into the natural opening of the duct in your mouth. The opening is usually gently stretched to let it in. Very occasionally a small cut is needed if the opening is very tight. The duct is rinsed with fluid as the surgeon looks inside.

  • Small stones can be removed through the telescope. Larger stones can sometimes be broken into smaller pieces first.
  • Narrowings (strictures) can be stretched to improve flow.
  • The gland can be washed with saline or medicines such as steroids or antibiotics.
  • It is usually done under local anaesthetic in the out-patient department. Occasionally a general anaesthetic is used if it forms part of another procedure.
  • A test only takes around 20 to 30 minutes. A treatment can take up to 45 minutes.

Afterwards the gland is often swollen for up to 24 hours because of the rinsing. Simple painkillers help. Eat a soft, bland diet. Gentle massage may help the swelling settle if your surgeon advises it. Most people are back to normal the next day.

Risks of sialendoscopy

  • Bleeding: very unlikely.
  • Infection: uncommon. Antibiotics are used if your surgeon is concerned.
  • Nerve injury: very unlikely with the telescope alone. A small risk exists if it is combined with open surgery.

The benefit is that the aim is to cure your symptoms without removing the gland. There is good evidence that the gland recovers its function once the blockage is removed. Not all stones can be removed this way. Large stones, or stones stuck within the gland, may need another approach.

Removing the stone through the mouth

A stone near the front of the duct can be removed through a small cut inside the mouth. This is usually straightforward but carries a risk of numbness of the tongue. Any stitches used inside the mouth are dissolvable.

Removing the gland

If large stones are stuck within the gland, or infection and swelling keep coming back, removal of the gland may be recommended. This gives a lasting answer to the problem but carries risks of a scar and possible nerve injury. The nerves at risk depend on which gland is removed. It is done under general anaesthetic. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

For the submandibular gland, a cut is made in the upper neck below the jaw. Most people stay 24 to 48 hours and a small drain is occasionally used. Risks include a blood clot under the skin (about 1 in 20, around 5%), numbness of the skin, weakness of the lower lip (usually temporary, lasting 2 to 3 months), and numbness of the tongue (usually temporary). You are very unlikely to have a dry mouth afterwards. Most people take around 2 weeks off work. Stitches are dissolvable.

5. What is the outlook?

Most people do well. Many stones can be removed or passed, and the gland usually returns to normal. Some people form more stones in the future, so keep drinking enough water and tell us if swelling returns. If a gland has to be removed, the other glands keep your mouth moist.

Occasionally a lump turns out to be a cancer once it has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

Questions patients often ask

Will the stone come back?

Some people form further stones. Staying well hydrated and acting early on swelling helps. Tell your team if it returns.

Can I eat before a sialogram?

Yes. You may eat, drink and take your usual medicines beforehand.

Can I go to work after sialendoscopy?

You may need the rest of the day off. Most people are back to normal the following day.

Do I have to lose my gland?

Not usually. Most stones can be treated by keeping the gland. Removal is only considered if other treatments fail.
