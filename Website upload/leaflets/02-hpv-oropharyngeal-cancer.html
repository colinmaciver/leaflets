HPV-Related Throat (Oropharyngeal) Cancer

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains cancers at the back of the mouth and throat that are linked to the human papillomavirus (HPV). Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team will explain what it means for you.

At a glance
  • HPV is a very common virus. Some high-risk types can cause cancer of the tonsils and the base of the tongue.
  • Most people clear the virus without ever knowing they had it. Cancer develops in only a very small number, usually many years later.
  • Common first signs are a painless lump in the neck, a sore throat on one side that does not settle, or difficulty swallowing.
  • HPV-related throat cancer usually responds very well to treatment, and the outlook is generally better than for other throat cancers.
  • You have not done anything wrong. Having HPV is not something to be ashamed of.

1. What is HPV and how is it linked to throat cancer?

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a very common virus. More than 100 types are known. It affects the skin and the moist lining of the body, such as the inside of the mouth, throat and genital area. Most adults will catch HPV at some point in their lives, and in nearly everyone the body clears it within one to two years without any symptoms.

A few high-risk types, mainly HPV type 16, can stay in the body. Over many years, usually decades, these can change the cells and lead to cancer. The back of the throat (the oropharynx) is one of the areas affected. It includes the tonsils, the base of the tongue and the soft palate. Cancers here are called oropharyngeal cancers and are part of the group known as head and neck cancers.

HPV-related throat cancers have become more common in many countries over the last 20 years. In parts of Northern Europe and North America, most cancers of the oropharynx are now linked to HPV. In other regions the share is much lower. HPV is also linked to some cancers of the cervix, vulva, vagina, penis and anus.

Who is affected?

The cancer is more common in men and often affects people in their 40s, 50s and 60s, including many who have never smoked. Smoking and heavy drinking still raise the risk and can make treatment less effective. HPV spreads mostly through close skin and intimate contact, including oral sex. Because the virus can stay quiet for many years, it is usually impossible to say when or from whom someone caught it. It does not mean that you or your partner has been unfaithful.

2. What are the symptoms and how is it assessed?

Possible symptoms

  • A painless lump in the side of the neck. This is often the first sign.
  • A sore throat or earache on one side that does not go away
  • Difficulty or pain when swallowing, or a feeling of something stuck in the throat
  • A change in your voice
  • A lump or ulcer at the back of the mouth
  • Unexplained weight loss or tiredness

These can have many harmless causes. If any last longer than 3 weeks, please see your GP or dentist.

Tests

  • Examination. Your surgeon feels your neck and examines your mouth and throat, often with a thin flexible camera passed through the nose. This is done with a numbing spray and is not painful.
  • Biopsy. A small sample is taken, sometimes with a fine needle from a neck lump, to confirm the diagnosis. A needle test is done in clinic. A biopsy from the throat may need a short general anaesthetic.
  • HPV testing. The sample is tested for signs of HPV, usually with a marker called p16. This helps with planning treatment and with predicting how well it will work.
  • Scans. CT, MRI and often PET-CT scans show the size of the cancer and whether it has spread to lymph nodes in the neck or elsewhere.

Your results are discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting. Specialists from different fields agree together on the most appropriate plan for you.

Being told you have cancer, and that a virus is involved, can feel overwhelming. Many people feel worried, embarrassed or angry. These feelings are normal. Please ask us any question you like, however awkward it seems.

3. What are the treatment options?

HPV-related cancers usually respond well to treatment. The choice depends on the size of the cancer, whether lymph nodes are involved, and your general health. Your team will explain the choices and their side effects so that you can decide together.

Radiotherapy, with or without chemotherapy

Radiotherapy, often combined with chemotherapy (chemoradiotherapy), is a common treatment, particularly for larger cancers or when lymph nodes are involved. It is given daily on weekdays over around 6 to 7 weeks. It aims to cure the cancer while keeping your throat working. Side effects include a very sore mouth and throat, difficulty swallowing, taste change, dry mouth and tiredness. A feeding tube is sometimes needed for a time. Dietitians and speech and language therapists will help you.

Surgery

For some smaller cancers, surgery can be an alternative. The cancer in the tonsil or tongue base is removed through the mouth using specialist techniques, so no cut on the face or neck is needed for the cancer itself. A neck dissection (removing lymph nodes from the neck) is often done at the same time. Further radiotherapy may be advised depending on what is found under the microscope.

Which is better?

For small HPV-related cancers, surgery and radiotherapy can give similar chances of cure. The difference is in the side effects. Your team will talk you through what matters most to you, such as swallowing, speech, voice and dry mouth. Because people with HPV-related cancers often do very well, researchers are studying whether some treatment can safely be reduced to cut long-term side effects. Treatment should only be reduced in the setting of a clinical trial or an agreed plan from your MDT.

Advanced or returning cancer

If the cancer has spread or comes back, other options include further surgery, more radiotherapy, chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Your oncologist will explain these if they are relevant.

Looking after yourself during treatment

  • Stop smoking and cut down alcohol. This improves the chance of cure and reduces side effects.
  • See your dentist before radiotherapy. Teeth in the treated area may need care or removal.
  • Keep your mouth clean, and do your swallowing exercises as advised.

4. What is the outlook?

People with HPV-related throat cancer generally do better than those whose cancer is not linked to HPV. In large international studies, about 8 to 9 in 10 people (80 to 90%) with HPV-related oropharyngeal cancer are alive five years after treatment, and many are cured. The figure is lower, about 6 in 10, for the most advanced cancers. For all oropharyngeal cancers together in England (HPV-related or not), about 65 in 100 are alive five years after diagnosis. The exact outlook depends on the stage of the cancer and on whether you smoke or have smoked heavily. Your surgeon or oncologist will discuss your own outlook with you.

After treatment you will be seen regularly in clinic, usually for at least five years, to check for any return of the cancer and to help with side effects. Please contact us if you notice a new lump, a new sore throat or new swallowing problems.

Questions about partners, family and prevention

  • There is no routine test for HPV in the mouth or throat for partners.
  • Most partners have already been exposed to HPV and have cleared it. Treatment does not need to be delayed, and there is no need to avoid normal contact.
  • In the UK the HPV vaccine is offered to school-age children and some adults, and it protects against the high-risk types. If you are not sure whether you or your family are covered, ask your GP.
  • Condoms and dental dams reduce but do not completely remove the chance of passing on HPV.

Questions patients often ask

Did I catch this from my partner?

It is very unlikely to be possible to know. HPV can stay silent for decades before it causes any problem. A diagnosis does not mean that you or a partner has been unfaithful.

Is HPV-related throat cancer curable?

Often, yes. HPV-related cancers respond well to treatment and the cure rate is higher than for most other throat cancers. Your team will tell you how this applies to you.

Will my partner or family be at risk?

Your partner will almost certainly have already been exposed to HPV and will have cleared it. Cancer from HPV is uncommon and having a partner with this cancer does not meaningfully raise their own risk.

Should I be vaccinated against HPV?

The vaccine protects against future infection, not against cancer that is already present. Please ask your GP or team whether it is relevant for you or your family.
