Laryngeal (Voice Box) Cancer

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains what cancer of the voice box (larynx) is, how it is diagnosed and how it is treated. It is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team. Your own plan will be agreed with you in person.

At a glance
  • The most common early sign is a hoarse voice that lasts more than a few weeks.
  • Smoking and heavy alcohol use are the main causes. Most people are over 55.
  • Diagnosis uses a camera test in clinic and a biopsy, often under a short general anaesthetic, with scans.
  • Early cancers are often treated with radiotherapy or with keyhole surgery through the mouth. Larger cancers may need surgery, chemoradiotherapy or both.
  • Teams work hard to protect your voice and swallowing, and help is available if these change.

1. What is laryngeal cancer and why does it happen?

The larynx (voice box) sits at the top of the windpipe. It lets air pass into the lungs, makes the sound of your voice with the vocal cords, and has a flap (the epiglottis) that closes over the airway when you swallow so food does not go into the lungs. Many lumps in the larynx, such as vocal cord nodules or polyps, are not cancer.

Cancer happens when cells become abnormal, grow in an uncontrolled way and can spread. Most voice box cancers start in the lining cells (squamous cell carcinoma).

What causes it?

  • Smoking (cigarettes, cigars, pipes and chewing tobacco) is the main cause. Current smokers have about 10 times the risk of laryngeal cancer of people who have never smoked, and smoking is thought to cause about 6 in 10 (64%) laryngeal cancers in the UK.
  • Heavy alcohol use over many years, especially together with smoking. Alcohol is thought to cause about 2 in 10 (22%) laryngeal cancers in the UK, and heavy smoking and heavy drinking together raise the risk much more than either alone.
  • Other factors: age (most are 55 or over), being male, a close family member with the cancer, a weakened immune system and exposure to asbestos.

Possible symptoms

  • Hoarseness or a change in your voice. This is the commonest early sign.
  • Pain or difficulty when swallowing.
  • A sore throat or pain in the ear, often on one side.
  • A lump in the neck.
  • Noisy or difficult breathing.

Most people with these symptoms do not have cancer, but if any last more than a few weeks (hoarseness especially), please have them checked.

2. How is it diagnosed?

Your surgeon will ask about your symptoms and health, including smoking and alcohol, and examine your mouth, throat and neck. Not everyone needs every test.

  • Nasoendoscopy (flexible laryngoscopy): a thin flexible camera passed through the nose, done in clinic with local anaesthetic spray. It shows the larynx and how well the vocal cords move.
  • Biopsy (microlaryngoscopy): a small sample is taken from the larynx through the open mouth under general anaesthetic. It also maps the cancer accurately. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. There may be some bleeding afterwards, and you may need to stop blood thinners beforehand.
  • Needle biopsy (FNA) of a neck lump, guided by ultrasound.
  • CT, MRI and PET scans show the size of the cancer, whether the voice box cartilage is involved, and whether it has spread.

Staging

Staging describes the size of the cancer and whether it has spread to lymph nodes or elsewhere, using the letters T (tumour), N (nodes) and M (spread to other parts of the body). Stages I and II are early and small. Stages III and IV are more advanced. Many people with more advanced cancers are still cured.

3. Your specialist team

Your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

The team includes head and neck surgeons, reconstructive surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, oncologists (cancer doctors who give radiotherapy and chemotherapy), specialist nurses, speech and swallowing therapists, dietitians, dental and oral medicine specialists, and psychological and social support. You are always welcome to ask for a second opinion.

4. Treatment options

The best treatment depends on where in the larynx the cancer is, its size, whether it has spread, your general health, your voice and swallowing, and your wishes. The two main treatments are radiotherapy and surgery. Chemotherapy is sometimes given at the same time as radiotherapy.

Early cancers (stage I and II)

Small cancers can often be cured by radiotherapy alone, given as a short daily outpatient course, or by removing the cancer through the mouth with a laser or robot (transoral surgery) without any cuts on the outside. Both have good results and aim to keep the voice. Your team will explain the pros and cons of each for you.

Larger cancers (stage III and IV)

  • Radiotherapy with chemotherapy (chemoradiotherapy) aims to cure the cancer and keep the voice box. Radiotherapy is given daily on weekdays for about 7 weeks. If the cancer does not clear or returns, surgery can still be done as a back-up.
  • Transoral laser or robotic surgery can remove some advanced cancers without external cuts.
  • Partial laryngectomy or pharyngectomy: removal of part of the voice box via the neck.
  • Total laryngectomy: removal of the whole voice box. You then breathe through a permanent opening in the neck (a stoma), and no longer breathe through your nose and mouth. Speech can be restored in several ways, which your speech and swallowing therapist will explain, including a small voice valve and electronic devices.
  • Neck dissection: removal of lymph nodes from the neck, even when scans do not show cancer there, because tiny deposits can be missed.
  • Free flap reconstruction: if a larger area of tissue is removed, skin and muscle (and sometimes bone) with their blood supply are taken from another part of the body and used to rebuild the area. In published studies about 5 in 100 flaps (around 5%; most reports give between 3 and 6 in 100) do not survive and need further surgery.
  • Temporary tracheostomy: a breathing tube placed in the windpipe through a small opening in the lower neck, used only after bigger operations while swelling settles. It is usually removed within about a week once normal breathing is safe.
  • Feeding tubes: a tube through the nose (nasogastric) for days or weeks, or a tube through the skin into the stomach (gastrostomy or PEG) if feeding support is needed for longer.

Surgery is done under general anaesthetic. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. A small drain is occasionally used, and stitches are dissolvable. Your surgeon will tell you how long you will be in hospital, which depends on the operation. After larger operations you are looked after in a high-dependency or intensive care unit at first.

Radiotherapy after surgery

If the final pathology shows a higher risk of the cancer coming back, radiotherapy (with or without chemotherapy) is usually given about 4 weeks after surgery and lasts about 6 weeks.

Side effects of radiotherapy

  • tiredness
  • sore, red or dry skin in the treated area
  • a sore mouth and throat, and pain on swallowing
  • dry mouth and thickened saliva
  • changes in taste, and a loss of appetite and weight
  • a hoarse voice

Most side effects start around two weeks into treatment, are at their worst just after it finishes and improve over the following 4 to 6 weeks. Some can last for months and a few may be permanent, such as a dry mouth. Because radiotherapy can affect the jaw bone, any broken or infected teeth are usually treated before it starts, and regular dental check-ups afterwards are important.

Less often, food or fluid can trickle into the windpipe, causing coughing or chest infection, or swelling can narrow the airway. Your team watches for these and can treat them. Your voice, swallowing and nutrition are supported throughout by a speech and swallowing therapist and dietitian.

5. Follow-up and outlook

After treatment you will have regular check-ups, including examination and camera checks of the throat. After chemoradiotherapy for a larger cancer, a PET scan is often arranged about 12 weeks after treatment to check the cancer has gone. Scans are not otherwise routine unless there is a concern.

The outlook depends on the stage, the exact site in the larynx, the treatment and your general health. Cancers found early have very good cure rates, and many people with larger cancers are cured too. Your team will talk through what this means for you in a personal and honest conversation.

If you smoke or drink alcohol, stopping or cutting down helps your recovery and lowers the chance of a new cancer. Ask the team for help. Support is available from your dietitian, speech and swallowing therapist, dentist, counsellor and social worker. Feeling anxious or low is very common. Please tell us if you are struggling.

Questions patients often ask

Will I lose my voice?

Many people keep their voice, especially with early cancers. If the voice box has to be removed, there are good ways to speak again and you will have a speech and swallowing therapist from the start.

Is radiotherapy or surgery better for an early cancer?

Both can cure it. Your team will explain which suits you best, and your preference matters.

Will I be able to eat normally?

Swallowing is often sore during and after treatment and improves for most people. Some need a feeding tube for a while. Your dietitian will guide you.

Should I stop smoking before treatment?

Yes. It helps healing and treatment work better, and lowers the chance of another cancer. We can help.
