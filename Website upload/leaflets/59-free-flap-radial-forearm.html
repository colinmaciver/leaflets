Free Flap Reconstruction — Radial Forearm

Using thin, pliable tissue from your forearm, with its own blood supply, to rebuild an area after major surgery

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has this been recommended?

When soft tissue is removed during head and neck surgery — for example as part of surgery for mouth or throat cancer — reconstruction is often needed to rebuild the area and restore function such as speech and swallowing. A radial forearm free flap uses a thin, pliable piece of skin and soft tissue from the underside of the forearm, based on the radial artery (one of the two main arteries supplying the hand), to rebuild the defect. Its thinness and flexibility make it particularly well suited to areas needing fine movement and shape, such as the tongue, floor of mouth, or cheek lining.

Before your operation, the blood supply to your hand is checked (usually with a simple bedside test and sometimes a scan) to confirm that the other artery supplying your hand can safely take over full blood supply once the radial artery is used for the flap. This is 'free' tissue transfer (a free flap), meaning the tissue is completely disconnected from the forearm, moved to the head and neck, and its blood vessels are then rejoined under magnification to vessels in the neck. A second surgical team usually raises the flap from your forearm at the same time as your defect is being prepared elsewhere, so the two parts of the operation often run in parallel to keep total surgery time as short as possible.

2. What does the anaesthetic involve?

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable. Combining reconstruction with resection of the original problem is major surgery and commonly takes several hours, and most patients spend time in a high-dependency or intensive care unit afterwards for close monitoring.

3. What does the procedure involve?

  • A piece of skin and underlying tissue is marked and raised from the underside of the forearm, based on the radial artery and its accompanying veins.
  • The flap is raised carefully towards the elbow, and the radial artery and veins are then divided, freeing the flap completely from the forearm.
  • The flap is moved to the head and neck and shaped to fit the defect.
  • Under surgical loupe magnification, the flap's own artery and vein are carefully joined to blood vessels in your neck, restoring its blood supply in its new position.
  • Once connected, the flap's blood flow is monitored very closely, usually with checks every hour for the first day or two, since a healthy blood supply is essential for it to survive.
  • The forearm donor site is usually covered with a split-thickness skin graft (see the separate Skin Grafting leaflet), taken from your thigh, since the defect is generally too wide to close directly; your arm is then supported in a splint or protective dressing for a period afterwards.

4. What is the expected outcome?

Immediately afterwards

  • Most patients spend the first one to two days in a high-dependency or intensive care unit for close monitoring of the flap's blood supply.
  • Your forearm will be splinted or supported to protect the skin graft while it takes, and you will be shown how to care for it.

As you recover

  • Swelling and bruising at the forearm and the reconstructed site settle over several weeks, and stitches dissolve on their own.
  • The skin graft on your forearm takes over the first one to two weeks and gradually settles in colour and texture, leaving a visible but usually flat scar.
  • Numbness or altered sensation over part of the hand or forearm is common, as a small sensory nerve running near the flap area can be affected — this can improve gradually but is sometimes permanent.
  • Hand and wrist movement is usually well preserved, though some patients notice their grip feels a little less strong than before, along with mild cold intolerance in the hand afterwards.
  • Your progress is followed up regularly, and if this reconstruction was combined with cancer surgery, a follow-up appointment is arranged to discuss the pathology results and any further treatment needed.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications are possible, but the great majority of patients recover well from this operation, and your team monitors closely for these and has clear plans to manage them. The approximate figure below is drawn from Mr Maciver's own surgical experience:

  • Pain, swelling, bleeding, and infection — expected to some degree at both the reconstructed site and the forearm donor site, and settling with simple measures if they occur.
  • Flap failure — the flap loses part or all of its blood supply and does not survive, occurring in approximately 4% of cases based on Mr Maciver's own outcomes data. This most often becomes apparent in the first few days, which is why monitoring is so frequent, and may require an urgent return to theatre or, occasionally, an alternative form of reconstruction.
  • A collection of blood or fluid under the flap or at the donor site (a haematoma or seroma), which may need drainage.
  • Donor site problems, including partial or complete loss of the skin graft (which can expose the underlying tendons and delay healing), a visible forearm scar, numbness over part of the hand, and, rarely, reduced circulation or cold sensitivity in the hand.
  • General risks of a longer operation and anaesthetic, including chest infection and blood clots in the leg (DVT) or lung (pulmonary embolism), which your anaesthetic assessment will take into account.
  • If this reconstruction was combined with cancer surgery, occasionally the final pathology is different or more extensive than expected once the tissue has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.
  • Further surgery may occasionally be needed to manage a complication, repeat a graft that has not taken, or adjust the flap's shape or bulk.
Your surgical team monitors closely for these complications and has clear plans in place to manage them if they occur. Please raise any worries with your team — they are used to these questions and would rather you ask.

Questions patients often ask

Why is tissue being taken from my forearm?

The underside of the forearm provides thin, pliable skin on a reliable blood vessel, which is well suited to rebuilding areas needing fine movement, such as the tongue or floor of mouth. Your surgeon will explain why this donor site has been chosen for your reconstruction.

Is it safe to use an artery from my arm?

Yes — before surgery, your hand's blood supply is checked to confirm that the other artery supplying it can safely take over full blood flow once the radial artery is used for the flap. Most people have two arteries supplying the hand for exactly this kind of redundancy.

Will I have a visible scar on my arm?

Yes, the forearm donor site usually needs a skin graft and leaves a visible, flat scar, and the colour and texture will differ somewhat from the surrounding skin. Your surgeon can discuss what to expect for your specific case.

Will my hand work normally afterwards?

Most patients keep good hand movement, though some numbness over part of the hand, a mild decrease in grip strength, and sensitivity to cold are all fairly common and can persist. Serious circulation problems are rare because of the pre-operative check of your hand's blood supply.

What happens if the flap fails?

This is uncommon (around 4% of cases based on Mr Maciver's own outcomes data) but is treated as an emergency if it happens, usually meaning an urgent return to theatre to try to restore blood flow, or occasionally a different form of reconstruction. Your team monitors closely specifically to catch this early.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, or any change in the colour or warmth of the reconstructed area, your hand, or your forearm. Use the contact details below.
