Facelift

Patient information leaflet - including MACS lift, mini facelift, traditional facelift and deep plane facelift

This leaflet explains facelift surgery, the main types of facelift and what to expect, so that you can talk it through with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team.

At a glance
  • A facelift lifts and tightens the deeper tissues of the lower face and neck and removes some loose skin. It does not stop ageing and it does not change the shape of your face.
  • There are several techniques (MACS lift, mini facelift, traditional facelift, deep plane facelift). The right one depends on your face, your goals and your health.
  • Most people need about two weeks off work. Bruising and swelling settle over 2 to 3 weeks; the final result takes several months.
  • The main risks are bruising and blood collections (haematoma), scarring, numbness, and, rarely, weakness of a facial nerve. Smoking greatly raises the risk of skin healing problems.

1. Why a facelift has been suggested

With age, the skin loses its stretch and the deeper tissues of the face loosen and sag. Fat shifts downwards. This leads to jowls, deep lines from the nose to the mouth, a less defined jawline and loose skin and bands in the neck.

A facelift repositions the deeper layers of the face (a layer called the SMAS, short for superficial musculo-aponeurotic system) and removes loose skin. The aim is a more rested, natural look. It is a cosmetic operation, so the decision is yours and it should never feel rushed.

What a facelift does not do

  • It does not treat fine wrinkles, sun damage or skin colour. Skin treatments, lasers or injections do this.
  • It does not lift the brows or treat the eyelids. These are separate operations (see the brow lift and eyelid surgery leaflets).
  • It does not stop the ageing process. Your face will keep ageing, but it will usually look younger than it would have done without surgery.
  • It does not give you a different face or the face of someone else.

Is a facelift right for me?

Good candidates are in reasonable general health, do not smoke, and have realistic expectations. Your surgeon will ask about your medical history, medicines (especially blood thinners, aspirin and herbal remedies), previous surgery and smoking. You will be asked to stop smoking and nicotine products (including vaping and patches) well before surgery, because they reduce the blood supply to the skin and can cause skin loss.

2. The anaesthetic

A full facelift is usually done under general anaesthetic (you are fully asleep). A smaller procedure, such as a mini facelift, can sometimes be done under local anaesthetic with sedation.

Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic - this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

You will not be able to drive or operate machinery for 48 hours after a general anaesthetic.

3. What the operation involves

The operation usually takes between 2 and 5 hours, depending on the technique and whether the neck is treated at the same time. The incisions are placed to hide as well as possible. They usually start in the hair above the ear, run in front of the ear (often along the natural fold or just inside the edge of the ear), go around the earlobe and finish behind the ear in the hairline. Your surgeon will mark these with you.

Skin is lifted, the deeper tissues are tightened or repositioned, extra skin is trimmed and the wounds are closed. When the neck needs work, the neck muscle (platysma) is also tightened, sometimes through a small cut under the chin.

Stitches and dressings

Most stitches under the skin are dissolvable. Stitches or clips in the hairline or behind the ear are sometimes non-dissolvable and are removed after about 5 to 10 days. Your surgeon will confirm which type of stitch is used. A light head dressing is often applied and a small drain is occasionally used; if so, it is usually removed within 24 to 48 hours.

4. What to expect afterwards

In hospital

Many people stay one night. You will be sat upright, given pain relief and sometimes antibiotics. Keeping your blood pressure well controlled helps to prevent bleeding, and your team may set a target for this. Do tell the nurses straight away if one side of your face becomes tight, swollen or painful, as this can mean bleeding.

Recovery at home

  • Sleep with your head raised on two or three pillows for the first 1 to 2 weeks. Avoid sleeping on your side.
  • Cold compresses (wrapped in a cloth, not on the skin) in the first 48 hours can ease swelling.
  • Expect swelling, bruising and tightness. These are worst in the first 3 days and mostly settle over 2 to 3 weeks. Some swelling can last for several weeks.
  • The skin of the cheeks and around the ears will feel numb or odd. This improves over weeks to months.
  • Do not turn your head sharply, pull on the incisions or wear tight collars. Be gentle when washing and drying your hair; your surgeon will tell you when you can wash.
  • Avoid heavy lifting, bending and strenuous exercise for about 3 to 4 weeks, or as advised.
  • Most people return to desk work after about 2 weeks. Many are happy to be seen socially after 3 to 4 weeks.

Looking after the scars

Once the wounds have healed, gentle moisturiser massage may help the scars to settle, and strong sun protection (SPF 30 to 50) protects them from darkening. Your surgeon will advise you on this. Scars are usually pink for some months and fade to a fine line, often hidden in the hairline and natural folds.

Follow-up

You will be seen in clinic after about a week for a wound check. The full result takes 3 to 6 months to appear, once swelling has gone.

5. Possible complications

All surgery carries risk. Facelift is generally safe in healthy people. The figures below come from large published series and are approximate. Your own risk depends on your health, whether you smoke and the technique used.

Common and expected

  • Pain, swelling, bruising and tightness: expected and settle over weeks.
  • Numbness or altered feeling of the cheeks, ears and neck: common and usually improves over months. A small patch of numbness can last longer.
  • Scarring: you will have scars. Most fade well, but some can be wide, thick or red.
  • Tightness of the neck or some visible bands that can be slow to soften.

Less common

  • Bleeding and haematoma (a collection of blood under the skin): the most common serious complication, in about 2 to 3 in 100 patients (2 to 3%) in a 2025 pooled analysis of 47 studies, and about 1 in 100 (1.1%) in the largest single series of over 11,000 facelifts. It is more common in men and in people with high blood pressure. It usually needs a return to theatre to drain it.
  • Infection: uncommon, about 3 in 1,000 (0.3%) in the large series of over 11,000 facelifts, and low in pooled reviews. Treated with antibiotics.
  • Delayed healing or skin loss (necrosis), especially behind the ear: much more likely in smokers, in whom the risk is several times higher. It can leave a wider scar.
  • Hair loss next to the scars, and a change in the position of the hairline or sideburn. Usually temporary but can be permanent.
  • Facial nerve injury (weakness of part of the face): pooled figures from a recent review of studies show about 1 in 150 (around 0.7%) have some weakness, and almost all of these recover. Permanent weakness is very rare (less than 1 in 1,000).
  • Sensory nerve injury: a small area of ongoing numbness, especially of the ear, is possible. Some sensory nerve damage was found in about 4 in 1,000 patients (0.4%) in a 2025 pooled review, and it was permanent in fewer than 1 in 1,000.
  • Fluid collection (seroma), contour irregularities, asymmetry and dimpling: these may need minor treatment.
  • Dissatisfaction with the result, or a result that does not last as long as hoped. Further surgery is sometimes needed.

Rare

  • Blood clots in the legs or lungs. Your team will take steps to reduce this risk.
  • Anaesthetic complications (discussed at your anaesthetic assessment).
Contact the clinic or the Emergency Department urgently if you have rapidly increasing swelling or pain on one side of your face, severe pain, a spreading redness, a fever, chest pain or breathlessness, or a leg that becomes swollen and painful.

Questions patients often ask

How long will a facelift last?

There is no fixed answer. The face keeps ageing, but most people still look younger than they would have without surgery for many years. Your skin quality, genetics, weight changes, sun exposure and smoking all matter. Some surgeons say deep plane techniques last longer, but good long-term comparison studies are limited.

Which type of facelift is best?

There is no single best operation. A mini facelift or MACS lift can be right for early changes; a traditional or deep plane facelift is usually needed for more marked sagging. Your surgeon will recommend the technique that suits your face.

Will people know I have had surgery?

The aim is a natural, refreshed look. After 3 to 4 weeks most bruising is gone and scars are well hidden by hair and natural folds.

Can I smoke before or after the operation?

No. Smoking and nicotine greatly increase the risk of skin loss and poor healing. You will be asked to stop well before surgery and not to restart afterwards.

Will it affect how I smile or move my face?

Temporary weakness is uncommon and almost always recovers. Permanent weakness is very rare.

Can the facelift be combined with other procedures?

Yes. It is often combined with a necklift, eyelid surgery, a brow lift or fat grafting. Your surgeon will discuss what is sensible for you.
