Jaw Joint Keyhole Surgery (TMJ Arthroscopy)

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains keyhole surgery of the jaw joint, why it is done and what to expect. Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team will talk it through with you.

At a glance
  • TMJ arthroscopy lets your surgeon look inside the jaw joint with a small camera and treat some problems at the same time.
  • It is done under general anaesthetic through a small cut in front of the ear.
  • It usually takes about 20 minutes for each joint, up to two hours if treatment inside the joint is needed.
  • You should be able to go home the same day.
  • Most people can return to work after a few days.

1. Why has this operation been recommended?

The jaw joint, or temporomandibular joint (TMJ), sits just in front of the ear. Some joint problems cannot be seen well on scans, such as a torn or displaced disc of cartilage, scarring, or damage to the joint surface. Your jaw may click, lock, hurt or not open well.

The aim of the operation is to find out exactly what the problem is and, for many people, to treat it at the same time.

Alternatives

Many jaw joint problems settle without an operation. Your surgeon will usually start with simple measures such as a soft diet and rest for the jaw, anti-inflammatory painkillers, treatment of teeth grinding (bruxism) including a soft splint made by your dentist, and sometimes anti-inflammatory gel, muscle-relaxing medicines, jaw exercises, Botox or a steroid injection into the joint. Scans (CT or MRI) are often used to look at the joint without surgery.

Arthroscopy is offered if these have not helped enough.

2. The anaesthetic

The operation is done under general anaesthetic, so you will be fully asleep. You must not drive or operate machinery for 48 hours afterwards.

Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

3. What does the operation involve?

A small cut is made in the skin in front of your ear. Your surgeon passes a thin camera (an arthroscope) into the joint to see inside. Depending on what is found, your surgeon may:

  • wash out the joint with fluid through one or two needles, removing loose material caused by wear of the joint surfaces,
  • use fine instruments to treat scarring or smooth the joint surfaces, or
  • place a stitch to change the position of the cartilage disc.

The operation usually takes about 20 minutes for each joint. It can take up to two hours if surgery is needed inside the joint. The small cut is closed with stitches, usually dissolvable ones, and a small dressing.

4. What to expect afterwards

  • Swelling and discomfort. These are usually worst in the first few hours. Painkillers will be arranged.
  • Going home. You should be able to go home the same day.
  • Work. You should be able to return to work after a few days, depending on your job.
  • Exercise. Regular activity helps you return to normal as soon as possible. Ask your team before starting exercise.
  • Jaw exercises. Your surgeon may give you a programme of jaw exercises. See our leaflet on jaw exercises after TMJ surgery.
  • Review. A follow-up appointment will be arranged to check your jaw and plan any further treatment.

5. Possible complications

These are uncommon. Published percentages vary, so they are described in words.

General

  • Pain.
  • Bleeding.
  • Bruising and swelling of the face.
  • Infection of the wound.
  • Blood clots in the legs (rare).

Specific to the jaw joint

  • Not being able to open the mouth fully (trismus) and jaw stiffness.
  • Tenderness and pain in the joint.
  • Numbness of the temple and ear, usually temporary.
  • Weak forehead movement. This is due to a nerve near the joint and is usually temporary.
  • The problem not improving, or coming back. Further treatment, including open joint surgery, may be discussed.

Questions patients often ask

Will I have a scar?

The cut is small and sits in front of the ear. It usually fades well.

Is it painful?

There is soreness and swelling, worst early on, and painkillers help.

Will it fix my jaw problem?

It helps many people but cannot be guaranteed. Your surgeon will explain what was found.
