Free Flap Reconstruction — Latissimus Dorsi

Using muscle, and sometimes skin, from your back, with its own blood supply, to rebuild an area after major surgery

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has this been recommended?

When a large area of tissue is removed during head and neck surgery, or when a defect needs a substantial amount of well-vascularised tissue to fill it, reconstruction is needed to rebuild the area and restore form and function. The latissimus dorsi is a large, flat muscle covering much of the back, and it can be taken — either as muscle alone, or together with an overlying paddle of skin — based on a single reliable set of blood vessels running to it under the arm. Its size and reliable blood supply make it particularly useful for large or complex defects, including bulky soft-tissue loss and, occasionally, situations needing extra coverage after previous reconstructions have failed.

This is 'free' tissue transfer (a free flap), meaning the muscle (with skin if needed) is completely disconnected from the back, moved to the head and neck, and its blood vessels are then rejoined under magnification to vessels in the neck. A second surgical team usually raises the flap from your back at the same time as your defect is being prepared elsewhere, so the two parts of the operation often run in parallel to keep total surgery time as short as possible.

2. What does the anaesthetic involve?

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable. You will be positioned on your side for the part of the operation raising the flap. Combining reconstruction with resection of the original problem is major surgery and commonly takes several hours, and most patients spend time in a high-dependency or intensive care unit afterwards for close monitoring.

3. What does the procedure involve?

  • You are positioned on your side, and an incision is made over the back overlying the latissimus dorsi muscle, taking a paddle of skin with it if this is needed for your reconstruction.
  • The muscle is raised and its blood vessels (the thoracodorsal artery and vein) are traced back towards the armpit, together with the nerve that supplies the muscle.
  • Once the pedicle is fully freed, the vessels are divided, completely disconnecting the flap from the back.
  • The flap is moved to the head and neck and shaped to fit the defect, using as much muscle and skin as your reconstruction requires.
  • Under surgical loupe magnification, the flap's own artery and vein are carefully joined to blood vessels in your neck, restoring its blood supply in its new position.
  • Once connected, the flap's blood flow is monitored very closely, usually with checks every hour for the first day or two, since a healthy blood supply is essential for it to survive.
  • The donor site on your back is usually closed directly with dissolvable stitches, and a drain is left in place for a period afterwards, since a sizeable space is left behind after such a large muscle is taken.

4. What is the expected outcome?

Immediately afterwards

  • Most patients spend the first one to two days in a high-dependency or intensive care unit for close monitoring of the flap's blood supply.
  • Your back will be bandaged and a drain will usually be in place for several days, sometimes longer than for smaller donor sites, to remove fluid while the space left behind by the muscle settles and heals.

As you recover

  • Swelling, bruising, and discomfort at the back and the reconstructed site settle over several weeks, and stitches dissolve on their own.
  • A build-up of fluid under the back wound (a seroma) is common with this donor site even after the drain is removed, and may need to be drawn off with a needle on one or more occasions in clinic — this is a routine part of recovery rather than a sign that something has gone wrong.
  • Some weakness in shoulder movements that rely on the latissimus dorsi muscle — particularly pulling the arm down and inward, such as in climbing or swimming strokes — is expected, though most patients do not notice a difference in everyday activities, since other muscles around the shoulder compensate.
  • The donor site scar, and the reconstructed area, continue to soften and fade over several months.
  • Your progress is followed up regularly, and if this reconstruction was combined with cancer surgery, a follow-up appointment is arranged to discuss the pathology results and any further treatment needed.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications are possible, but the great majority of patients recover well from this operation, and your team monitors closely for these and has clear plans to manage them. The approximate figure below is drawn from Mr Maciver's own surgical experience:

  • Pain, swelling, bleeding, and infection — expected to some degree at both the reconstructed site and the back donor site, and settling with simple measures if they occur.
  • Flap failure — the flap loses part or all of its blood supply and does not survive, occurring in approximately 4% of cases based on Mr Maciver's own outcomes data. This most often becomes apparent in the first few days, which is why monitoring is so frequent, and may require an urgent return to theatre or, occasionally, an alternative form of reconstruction.
  • A collection of blood or fluid under the flap or at the donor site (a haematoma or seroma), which is relatively common with this particular donor site and may need drainage on more than one occasion.
  • Donor site problems, including some loss of shoulder pulling strength (usually not noticeable for daily activities), altered sensation around the scar, and a visible scar on the back.
  • General risks of a longer operation and anaesthetic, including chest infection and blood clots in the leg (DVT) or lung (pulmonary embolism), which your anaesthetic assessment will take into account.
  • If this reconstruction was combined with cancer surgery, occasionally the final pathology is different or more extensive than expected once the tissue has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.
  • Further surgery may occasionally be needed to manage a complication or adjust the flap's shape or bulk.
Your surgical team monitors closely for these complications and has clear plans in place to manage them if they occur. Please raise any worries with your team — they are used to these questions and would rather you ask.

Questions patients often ask

Why is tissue being taken from my back?

The latissimus dorsi is a large muscle with a single reliable blood supply, able to provide a substantial amount of well-vascularised tissue, which is useful for larger or more complex defects. Your surgeon will explain why this donor site has been chosen for your reconstruction.

Will my shoulder work normally afterwards?

Most patients keep good, functional shoulder movement for everyday activities. Some loss of strength in pulling movements that specifically rely on the latissimus dorsi (such as climbing or certain swimming strokes) is expected, as other shoulder and back muscles largely compensate for the loss of this one muscle.

Will I have a visible scar on my back?

Yes, a scar remains at the donor site, generally positioned so it is reasonably well hidden by clothing and swimwear, and it typically fades and flattens over several months.

Why do I sometimes need fluid drained from my back after I go home?

A build-up of fluid (a seroma) in the space left behind after the muscle is taken is a well-recognised and common part of recovery from this particular donor site. It is usually managed simply, by drawing the fluid off with a needle in clinic, and does not mean anything has gone wrong.

What happens if the flap fails?

This is uncommon (around 4% of cases based on Mr Maciver's own outcomes data) but is treated as an emergency if it happens, usually meaning an urgent return to theatre to try to restore blood flow, or occasionally a different form of reconstruction. Your team monitors closely specifically to catch this early.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, or any change in the colour or warmth of the reconstructed area. Use the contact details below.
