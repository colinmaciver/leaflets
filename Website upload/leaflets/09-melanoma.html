Melanoma (Skin Cancer)

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains melanoma, the most serious type of skin cancer: what it is, how it is checked, how it is treated and how to protect your skin in future. It is written to support the conversations you will have with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team.

At a glance
  • Melanoma starts in the pigment cells (melanocytes) of the skin. It is much less common than other skin cancers but more likely to spread.
  • When it is found early, melanoma is usually cured by an operation to remove it with a margin of healthy skin.
  • The main factors that decide the plan are how thick the melanoma is (Breslow thickness), whether it is ulcerated, and whether it has reached the lymph nodes.
  • Newer treatments (immunotherapy and targeted drugs) have greatly improved the outlook for melanoma that has spread.
  • Protecting your skin from the sun and checking it regularly are the best ways to lower your risk.

1. What is melanoma and why does it happen?

The skin contains pigment-making cells called melanocytes. They give skin its colour and make more pigment when you are in the sun. Melanoma is a cancer that starts in these cells. It often appears as a new dark spot or a change in an existing mole, but some melanomas are pink or red and have little colour.

Melanoma is less common than basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, the other two main skin cancers, but it is more likely to spread to the lymph nodes and other parts of the body if it is not treated early. It can occur on the head and neck, on the arms, legs and body, and, less often, on the soles of the feet, under the nails or inside the mouth.

What increases the risk?

  • Ultraviolet (UV) light from the sun or sunbeds. Sunburn, especially in childhood and the teenage years, is an important cause.
  • Fair skin that burns easily, red or fair hair, and many freckles.
  • Having lots of moles (more than about 50), or unusual-looking moles.
  • A personal or family history of melanoma.
  • A weakened immune system, for example after an organ transplant.
  • Rare inherited conditions that affect skin repair or moles.

What should I look out for?

Check your skin regularly, including your scalp, face, ears, neck, back and the soles of your feet. Ask a partner or family member to look at places you cannot see. See your doctor promptly if you notice any of the following:

  • A new mole or dark patch, or one that is different from your other moles (the "ugly duckling").
  • A mole that changes in size, shape or colour, has an uneven edge, or has more than one colour.
  • A mole that becomes itchy, crusty, bleeds or does not heal.
  • A spot that is growing over weeks to months.

Most changing moles are not melanoma, but they should always be checked.

2. How is melanoma assessed?

Your doctor will ask about the spot, your sun exposure and your family history, and will examine your whole skin and the lymph nodes in your neck and around your ear. A hand-held magnifying device (a dermatoscope) is used to look closely at the spot.

Biopsy

The only sure way to diagnose melanoma is to examine the tissue under the microscope. Where possible, the whole spot is removed with a narrow rim of normal skin (an excision biopsy). If the area is too large to remove safely without reconstruction, a small sample is taken instead. This is done under local anaesthetic, so you feel no pain.

The pathologist reports several important features, including:

  • Breslow thickness, the depth of the melanoma in millimetres. This is the most important factor in predicting the outlook.
  • Whether the surface is ulcerated (broken down).
  • How fast the cells are dividing, and whether cancer cells are seen in small blood or lymph vessels or around nerves.
  • Whether the edges of the sample are clear of cancer.

Scans and lymph node tests

A thin or early melanoma may not need any scans. For thicker melanomas, or if there is any sign of spread, you may be offered a CT or PET-CT scan, and sometimes an MRI scan of the brain. If a lymph node in the neck or near the ear feels or looks abnormal, it will usually be checked with an ultrasound scan and a needle test. On the face, drainage from the skin can go to nodes near the ear, in the neck or in more than one area, so a special scan using a tiny amount of tracer (lymphoscintigraphy) is often used to map the drainage.

Team decision

Your results will be discussed at a skin cancer or head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting. Specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, which is then explained to you in person before treatment goes ahead.

3. Treatment: the operation

Surgery is the main treatment for melanoma.

Wide local excision

After the diagnosis, a second operation removes a margin of healthy-looking skin and the tissue just under it from around the scar or remaining melanoma. This reduces the chance of the melanoma coming back in the same place. The margin depends mainly on the thickness of the melanoma. In general it is about 1 cm for thinner melanomas and about 2 cm for thicker ones. On the face the surgeon may adjust the margin to protect important structures such as the eyelid, nose or lip.

Small areas can often be closed directly or with a local flap, where nearby skin is moved across. Larger areas may need a skin graft or a larger flap. Your surgeon will explain what is planned for you. Stitches are usually dissolvable, and your surgeon will confirm which type is used.

The anaesthetic

Small operations can be done under local anaesthetic. Larger operations are done under general anaesthetic. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

Sentinel lymph node biopsy

The sentinel node is the first lymph node that skin cancer cells would reach. For melanomas of medium thickness, or thinner ones with worrying features such as ulceration, you may be offered a sentinel node biopsy. A tracer is injected near the scar before the operation. During the same anaesthetic, the surgeon finds and removes the one or few nodes that take it up, and the pathologist checks them. A clear result is reassuring. If cancer is found, you will be offered further tests and treatment, and this is discussed at the MDT.

What to expect afterwards

  • Most people go home the same day. Some larger reconstructions need a short hospital stay.
  • Expect some soreness, swelling and bruising for one to two weeks. Simple painkillers are usually enough.
  • A dressing will protect the wound. Your team will tell you how long to keep it dry and when to start gentle scar care.
  • Avoid heavy lifting or straining for the first couple of weeks if the wound is on the head, neck or trunk.
  • A scar is unavoidable. It fades over many months. Protect it from the sun.

Possible complications

Most people recover without problems. Possible complications include bleeding, infection, wound opening, a visible or stretched scar, numbness around the scar, and, if a graft or flap is used, partial loss of the graft or flap. After a sentinel node biopsy, a small collection of fluid (seroma) or temporary numbness can occur, and rarely weakness of a nearby nerve. A very small number of patients have a skin reaction to the tracer dye. Your surgeon will go through the risks that apply to you.

4. Further treatment if the melanoma has spread

If the melanoma has reached the lymph nodes or elsewhere, treatment is planned around you by the MDT. Options include:

  • Neck or parotid lymph node surgery to remove nodes that contain cancer.
  • Immunotherapy. These drugs help your own immune system recognise and attack melanoma cells. They are given by drip and can be used after surgery to lower the risk of return, or for melanoma that has spread. They can cause side effects such as tiredness, rash, diarrhoea and inflammation of other organs, which must be reported quickly.
  • Targeted therapy. About half of melanomas have a change (mutation) in a gene called BRAF. If yours does, tablets that target it may be an option. Your tumour is tested for this if it is at a higher stage.
  • Radiotherapy, sometimes after node surgery or to control symptoms.
  • Clinical trials, if you are suitable and wish to take part.

These treatments are usually given in specialist cancer services, working closely with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team.

5. Expected outcome and follow-up

The outlook depends mostly on how thick the melanoma was and whether it had spread when it was found. The figures below are for England and describe groups of people, not individuals. Your own outlook may be better or worse.

Stage when diagnosedApproximate 5-year survival
Stage 1 (thin, no spread)Almost 100 in 100
Stage 2 (thicker, or ulcerated, no spread)About 85 in 100
Stage 3 (spread to lymph nodes or nearby skin)About 75 in 100
Stage 4 (spread to other organs)Improving with newer treatments; no standard figure yet

Follow-up visits are planned for several years, because melanoma can come back, and people who have had one melanoma are at higher risk of another. How often you are seen depends on the stage. Many people with thin melanomas are seen less often, and people with higher-stage melanoma are seen more often and may have scans. Between visits, check your skin and the lymph node areas of your neck, and report any new lump or change straight away.

Being told you have melanoma can be frightening. Please tell us if you are struggling. Support is available for you and your family.

6. Preventing melanoma and protecting your skin

  • Spend time in the shade when the sun is strong, especially in the middle of the day. In the UAE the UV level is high for most of the year.
  • Cover up with a wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses and loose clothing with a close weave.
  • Use sunscreen of at least SPF 30 and four-star UVA protection. Apply it generously, repeat every two hours and after swimming. Sunscreen is not a reason to stay out longer.
  • Do not use sunbeds or sunlamps.
  • Never let children burn. Keep babies out of direct sun.
  • Check your skin once a month, and take a photograph of moles you are unsure of to help you see changes.
  • Ask your doctor about a regular full skin check if you have lots of moles or a family history.

Vitamin D is made by the skin in sunlight. In a sunny climate, everyday outdoor exposure is usually enough. Ask your doctor before taking supplements.

Questions patients often ask

Is melanoma always caused by sun exposure?

Sunburn and UV exposure are the main cause, but not the only one. Some melanomas occur in places that rarely see the sun, and family history also plays a part.

Will I need a skin graft?

Many small melanomas can be closed directly. A graft or flap is used when the area removed is larger. Your surgeon will explain this before your operation.

Why do I need a second operation if the whole mole has been removed?

The first biopsy removes only a narrow rim. The wider excision removes the surrounding skin where a few cancer cells may remain, to lower the chance of the melanoma returning.

Could the melanoma come back?

Yes, which is why you will be followed up for several years. The risk is related to the thickness and other features reported by the pathologist.

Can my family get checked?

Close relatives of someone with melanoma have a slightly higher risk. They should protect their skin and have any changing moles checked.
