Anaesthesia Options for Oral and Facial Surgery

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains the three main types of anaesthesia used for oral and facial surgery and what to expect with each. Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team will help you choose.

At a glance
  • There are three main options: local anaesthetic, sedation with local anaesthetic, and general anaesthetic.
  • The choice depends on the operation, your health, your wishes and how anxious you feel.
  • You will be given fasting instructions if you are having sedation or general anaesthetic. Follow them carefully.
  • You are monitored throughout.
  • You will need an adult to take you home and stay with you after sedation or general anaesthetic.

1. What is anaesthesia and why is it needed?

Anaesthesia stops you feeling pain during an operation. Different types suit different operations. Some operations are done with only a numbing injection, while others need you to be asleep. Anaesthesia is provided by an anaesthetist (a doctor trained in anaesthesia), or, for some local anaesthetic and sedation procedures, by the oral and maxillofacial surgeon who is trained to do this.

Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

2. The options

Local anaesthetic

A numbing injection is given in the gum or skin. After a few minutes the area is numb. You stay awake and can feel touch and pressure but not pain. It is used for tooth extractions, small lumps and biopsies. You can usually drive and return to normal activities once the numbness has worn off, which takes a few hours. Avoid eating hot food or biting your lip or cheek until feeling returns.

Local anaesthetic with sedation

A sedative medicine is given into a vein in your arm (intravenous sedation), and sometimes as a gas or tablet. You feel relaxed and drowsy and are less aware of what is happening. You can usually respond when spoken to. Many people remember little. A local anaesthetic is also given so there is no pain. It is useful for anxious patients or longer procedures. You need someone to take you home and stay with you, and you must not drive, drink alcohol or sign important papers for 24 hours.

General anaesthetic

You are completely asleep and feel nothing. Medicines are given through a vein, or sometimes as a gas, and a breathing tube is placed (often through the nose in mouth surgery) while you sleep. It is used for bigger operations, for longer surgery, for children, and for people who cannot tolerate being awake. Many operations can be done as day cases. You must not drive, operate machinery or sign legal documents for 48 hours.

3. What does the process involve?

  • Assessment. The team checks your medical history, medicines, allergies, previous anaesthetics (including any family problems with anaesthetics), loose teeth or dentures, and may do tests. Tell us about smoking, alcohol and any recreational drugs.
  • Fasting. For sedation or general anaesthetic you will be told when to stop eating and drinking. Follow this exactly, because food in the stomach can be dangerous. Sometimes you may be allowed clear fluids up to a set time.
  • Medicines. You will be told which medicines to take and which to stop, for example blood thinners and diabetes medicines.
  • On the day. A needle is placed in your arm. Heart rate, oxygen and blood pressure are monitored throughout. The team will check your details and consent.

4. What to expect afterwards

  • After sedation or general anaesthetic you wake up in a recovery area, with nurses watching you closely.
  • You may feel drowsy, sick, have a dry or sore throat or a blocked nose. These usually pass quickly.
  • You will be given painkillers and advice. You must have a responsible adult to take you home.
  • After a general anaesthetic or sedation, rest for the rest of the day.

5. Risks and results

Modern anaesthesia is very safe, especially for fit people. Risks depend on your general health and the operation. Your anaesthetist will discuss your own risks with you.

  • Common and minor: nausea, drowsiness, sore throat, dry mouth, bruising where the needle went in.
  • Less common: headache, muscle aches, a short-lived confusion or memory problem, dental damage (rare, which is why loose teeth are checked), or a breathing problem needing treatment.
  • Rare: allergic reaction, awareness during general anaesthetic (very rare), serious heart or lung problems.
  • Local anaesthetic: temporary numbness lasting longer than expected, and in rare cases, a lasting change in feeling. Allergic reaction is rare.

If you have any concerns, discuss them with the team. Staff are trained to manage emergencies and to keep you safe throughout.

Questions patients often ask

Can I choose to be asleep for a simple extraction?

Sometimes, but sedation or general anaesthetic carries extra risks and is used when it is needed or clearly helpful. Your surgeon will discuss the options.

Will I feel anything during sedation?

You should feel relaxed and no pain. You may remember little or nothing.

Can I eat or drink beforehand?

For a local anaesthetic alone, yes, eat normally. For sedation or general anaesthetic, follow your fasting instructions.

Who will look after me?

A trained anaesthetist or sedation-trained surgeon and a team with monitoring equipment.
