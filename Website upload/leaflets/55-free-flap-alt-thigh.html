Free Flap Reconstruction — Anterolateral Thigh (ALT)

Using tissue from your thigh, with its own blood supply, to rebuild an area after major surgery

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has this been recommended?

When a significant amount of tissue is removed during head and neck surgery — for example as part of surgery for mouth or throat cancer — reconstruction is often needed to rebuild the area, restore function such as speech and swallowing, and support your appearance. An anterolateral thigh (ALT) free flap is one of the most versatile options for this: it provides a generous, pliable piece of skin and soft tissue, on a long, reliable blood vessel, making it well suited to rebuilding areas such as the tongue, floor of mouth, cheek lining, or large skin defects.

This is 'free' tissue transfer (a free flap), meaning the tissue is completely disconnected from the thigh, moved to the head and neck, and its blood vessels are then rejoined under magnification to vessels in the neck. This is different from a local flap, which stays attached to its original blood supply and simply moves into a nearby defect. A second surgical team usually raises the flap from your thigh at the same time as your tumour or defect is being prepared elsewhere, so the two parts of the operation often run in parallel to keep total surgery time as short as possible.

2. What does the anaesthetic involve?

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable. Combining reconstruction with resection of the original problem is major surgery and commonly takes several hours, and most patients spend time in a high-dependency or intensive care unit afterwards for close monitoring.

3. What does the procedure involve?

  • The front and outer side of the thigh is marked, and the blood vessels supplying the skin in this area (perforators) are identified.
  • A piece of skin and underlying tissue is raised on these vessels, tracing them back to their main artery and vein, which are then divided to free the flap completely from the thigh.
  • The flap is moved to the head and neck and shaped to fit the defect.
  • Under surgical loupe magnification, the flap's own artery and vein are carefully joined to blood vessels in your neck, restoring its blood supply in its new position.
  • Once connected, the flap's blood flow is monitored very closely, usually with checks every hour for the first day or two, since a healthy blood supply is essential for it to survive.
  • The donor site on your thigh is closed directly with dissolvable stitches if the flap was not too wide; for a wider flap, the donor site is instead covered with a split-thickness skin graft taken from elsewhere (see the separate Skin Grafting leaflet), and a small drain is usually left in the thigh for a few days.

4. What is the expected outcome?

Immediately afterwards

  • Most patients spend the first one to two days in a high-dependency or intensive care unit for close monitoring of the flap's blood supply.
  • Your thigh will be bandaged and a drain may be in place for a few days to remove excess fluid while the donor site heals.
  • You will be encouraged to begin mobilising with the support of the nursing and physiotherapy team once it is safe to do so — walking is usually well preserved after an ALT flap, since most of the underlying thigh muscle is left in place.

As you recover

  • Swelling and bruising in the thigh and at the reconstructed site settle over several weeks, and stitches dissolve on their own.
  • Numbness or altered sensation over part of the front of the thigh is common, as a small sensory nerve running through the area is often affected — this can improve gradually but is sometimes permanent.
  • The donor site scar, and the reconstructed area, continue to soften and fade over several months. A slight indentation where the tissue was taken is common and usually becomes less noticeable with time; camouflage make-up support is available if this is a concern to you.
  • Your progress is followed up regularly, and if this reconstruction was combined with cancer surgery, a follow-up appointment is arranged to discuss the pathology results and any further treatment needed.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications are possible, but the great majority of patients recover well from this operation, and your team monitors closely for these and has clear plans to manage them. The approximate figures below are taken from recent published studies (listed under the sources at the end):

  • Pain, swelling, bleeding, and infection — expected to some degree at both the reconstructed site and the thigh donor site, and settling with simple measures if they occur.
  • Flap failure — the flap loses part or all of its blood supply and does not survive. In published studies of head and neck free flaps this happens in about 5 in 100 cases (5%), with individual series ranging from about 1 to 10 in 100. A return to theatre for a blood-vessel problem is more common than outright failure (roughly 1 in 10 patients in published series), and many of these flaps are saved. This most often becomes apparent in the first few days, which is why monitoring is so frequent, and may require an urgent return to theatre or, occasionally, an alternative form of reconstruction.
  • A collection of blood or fluid under the flap or at the donor site (a haematoma or seroma), which may need drainage.
  • Donor site problems, including numbness or tingling over part of the front or outer thigh (common — about 1 in 4 patients in published reviews, and sometimes permanent), wound breakdown or problematic scarring (about 5 in 100), wound infection (about 2 in 100), and, uncommonly, weakness or delayed wound healing — particularly if the donor site required a skin graft.
  • A longer, more visible scar on the thigh than with smaller procedures, sometimes with a slight indentation where the tissue was taken, especially if a skin graft was needed to close it.
  • General risks of a longer operation and anaesthetic, including chest infection and blood clots in the leg (DVT) or lung (pulmonary embolism), which your anaesthetic assessment will take into account.
  • If this reconstruction was combined with cancer surgery, occasionally the final pathology is different or more extensive than expected once the tissue has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.
  • Further surgery may occasionally be needed to manage a complication, adjust the flap's shape or bulk, or address the donor site.
Your surgical team monitors closely for these complications and has clear plans in place to manage them if they occur. Please raise any worries with your team — they are used to these questions and would rather you ask.

Questions patients often ask

Why is tissue being taken from my thigh?

The anterolateral thigh provides a generous, pliable area of skin and soft tissue on a long, reliable blood vessel, which makes it well suited to rebuilding many head and neck defects. Your surgeon will explain why this particular donor site has been chosen for your reconstruction.

Will I be able to walk normally afterwards?

Most patients recover their normal walking ability, since the ALT flap uses mainly skin and fat and spares most of the underlying thigh muscle. Your physiotherapy team will support you in mobilising as soon as it is safe to do so.

Will my thigh feel normal again?

Numbness or altered sensation over part of the front of the thigh is common, because a small sensory nerve running through the area is often affected during flap harvest. This can improve over time but is sometimes permanent; it does not usually affect movement or strength.

How closely is the flap monitored?

Very closely, especially in the first few days — usually with checks of its colour, warmth, and blood flow every hour to begin with, since this is when any problem with its blood supply is most likely to show and can be treated quickly if caught early.

What happens if the flap fails?

This is uncommon (about 5 in 100, or 5%, in published studies of head and neck free flaps) but is treated as an emergency if it happens, usually meaning an urgent return to theatre to try to restore blood flow, or occasionally a different form of reconstruction. Your team monitors closely specifically to catch this early.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, or any change in the colour or warmth of the reconstructed area. Use the contact details below.
