Teeth and Mouth Care During and After Head and Neck Cancer Treatment

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains how to look after your teeth and mouth around treatment, including why a dental check before radiotherapy matters. It is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team.

At a glance
  • Radiotherapy and chemotherapy can cause a sore mouth, a dry mouth, taste changes and a lasting risk of tooth decay.
  • Have a dental check before radiotherapy starts. Teeth that are badly decayed, infected or loose may need to be removed first.
  • Radiotherapy to the jaw can reduce its blood supply, which can lead to bone damage (osteoradionecrosis), most often after a tooth extraction.
  • Always tell your dentist you have had head and neck radiotherapy, and check with your team before any extraction or implant.
  • Gentle daily care, fluoride and regular dental visits protect your teeth.

1. Why mouth care matters

Treatment for head and neck cancer can affect the lining of the mouth, the salivary glands, the teeth and the jaw. Looking after your mouth helps you eat, speak, heal and feel comfortable, and lowers the risk of serious problems later.

Common mouth problems

  • Mucositis (sore mouth). Radiotherapy and chemotherapy inflame and may ulcerate the lining of the mouth and throat. This can be painful and make swallowing hard, and increases the risk of infection. Pain relief and numbing mouthwashes can help. It usually heals within a couple of months after treatment ends.
  • Dry mouth (xerostomia). If the salivary glands are in the treated area, saliva may be reduced, sometimes permanently. This can make chewing, swallowing and tasting harder, and can lead to mouth thrush, ulcers from sharp teeth and loose dentures.
  • Tooth decay and gum disease. Saliva normally protects your teeth. Without enough of it, decay can be fast and aggressive, and is the biggest long-term risk.
  • Taste changes. Taste may be dulled or taste metallic. It usually recovers, but this can take many months and some changes may last.
  • Stiff jaw (trismus). Surgery or radiotherapy can make it hard to open your mouth fully. It can be temporary or long-lasting and affects eating, speech and cleaning your teeth. Jaw-stretching exercises from your team can help; do them regularly and mention any tightness early.

2. The dental check before radiotherapy

Your team will usually recommend a dental assessment before radiotherapy starts, with your own dentist or a dentist linked to the team. The dentist will examine your teeth and mouth, explain the likely effects, and plan how to protect your teeth.

Teeth that are heavily decayed, infected or loose (including from gum disease) are often best removed before radiotherapy begins. This is because irradiated bone and gum may heal poorly after an extraction, and because infection around a tooth raises the risk of osteoradionecrosis. Extractions are therefore done beforehand, where possible, with time to heal before radiotherapy starts.

Please make sure your dentist and OMFS team talk to each other. If you need an urgent dental review, tell your team so it can be arranged without delay.

3. Osteoradionecrosis (ORN): protecting your jaw

Radiotherapy to the jaw bone can slowly reduce its blood supply. In some people, the bone then struggles to heal and may die and become exposed (osteoradionecrosis). It can appear several months after treatment or many years later. Most cases are linked to a tooth extraction or other trauma, but it can also occur without one.

The risk is lifelong for the treated part of the jaw. Prevention depends on:

  • keeping your teeth and gums very healthy, with excellent daily care and regular dental visits,
  • telling every dentist that you have had radiotherapy to the head and neck,
  • seeking specialist advice from your OMFS team before any extraction, jaw surgery or dental implant in the treated area,
  • not smoking.
Contact your OMFS team promptly if you notice exposed bone in your mouth, a socket that is not healing, persistent jaw pain, numbness, swelling, or a bad taste or discharge from the jaw. Do not wait for it to settle.

4. Daily mouth and teeth care

  • Brush teeth, gums and tongue gently with a soft toothbrush after meals and at bedtime. Ask your dentist about a toothpaste with extra fluoride.
  • Clean between your teeth every day, as your dentist advises.
  • Rinse often with an alcohol-free mouthwash or a simple salt and bicarbonate of soda rinse (for example, a pinch of salt and about two teaspoons of bicarbonate of soda in half a litre of cooled boiled water). Avoid mouthwashes that contain alcohol.
  • Sip water regularly. Use dry-mouth gels, sprays or saliva substitutes, and a lip balm.
  • Avoid sugary and fizzy drinks, including sugar-free fizzy drinks, which can still harm teeth. Limit sugary and acidic foods, especially between meals. Your dietitian can help you eat enough without too much sugar.
  • Do not smoke, and avoid alcohol.
  • Clean dentures after meals and leave them out at night. If your mouth is sore, ask your team whether to leave them out for a while.

Check-ups

Regular dental review before, during and after treatment helps prevent problems. After radiotherapy, most people are advised to see their dentist about every three to four months at first, depending on their risk. Your dentist and OMFS team will advise you.

5. Expected outcome

Sore mouth and taste changes usually settle in the weeks and months after treatment. Dry mouth may improve a little but can be permanent if the salivary glands were in the treated area, so protecting your teeth is a lifelong task. With good care and early help, most people keep their teeth and avoid serious jaw problems.

Questions patients often ask

Why can't I just have a tooth out later if it causes trouble?

After radiotherapy, the jaw heals less well and extractions carry a higher risk of osteoradionecrosis. That is why problem teeth are best dealt with before treatment.

Will my saliva come back?

Saliva may recover partly over time, depending on how much of the salivary gland was treated. For some people the dryness is permanent.

Can I have dental implants after radiotherapy?

Sometimes, but this needs specialist assessment because of the risk to the jawbone. Please speak to your OMFS team before any implant work.

Is sugar-free chewing gum helpful?

Sugar-free gum or mints can stimulate saliva if you have some left. Check with your team that they are suitable for you.
