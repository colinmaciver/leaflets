Major Head and Neck Surgery: A General Overview

What to expect when your operation combines tumour removal, neck dissection, reconstruction, or a tracheostomy

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has this surgery been recommended?

Some conditions of the head and neck, particularly cancer, are best treated by combining several surgical steps into a single, longer operation rather than several separate ones. Depending on your specific condition, your operation may combine some or all of the following: removal of a tumour or affected tissue, a neck dissection (removal of lymph nodes at risk of containing cancer), reconstruction of the area using tissue moved from elsewhere in your body (a flap or graft), and a temporary tracheostomy (a breathing tube through the neck) to keep your airway safe while swelling settles.

This leaflet is a general orientation to what combined major head and neck surgery involves. Your surgeon will also give you a leaflet specific to your particular operation (for example, laryngectomy, maxillectomy, or surgery for oral cancer), which describes your individual procedure in more detail — this leaflet is best read alongside that one.

2. The anaesthetic

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable.

Because combined major head and neck surgery often involves more than one procedure performed together, operations commonly take between six and twelve hours, depending on the complexity of the resection and reconstruction planned. A longer combined operation carries a somewhat higher overall anaesthetic risk than a single, shorter procedure, which your anaesthetist will discuss with you as part of your assessment.

3. What does the procedure involve?

The components of the operation

  • Resection — removal of the tumour or affected tissue, with a margin of healthy tissue around it where the surgery is for cancer, to give the best chance of complete removal.
  • Neck dissection — removal of lymph node levels in the neck at risk of containing cancer cells, performed at the same time through the same or a linked incision (see the separate Neck Dissection leaflet for detail).
  • Reconstruction — rebuilding the area using local tissue, a skin graft, or a free flap (tissue moved from elsewhere in the body, such as the forearm, thigh, or shoulder blade area, reconnected to blood vessels in the neck using surgical loupe magnification). Reconstruction is tailored to what has been removed, and may rebuild soft tissue only, or bone and soft tissue together.
  • Tracheostomy — a temporary breathing tube placed through the front of the neck for some patients, to protect the airway while swelling from surgery settles; this is removed as soon as it is safe to do so.
  • A feeding tube — placed through the nose, or occasionally directly into the stomach, to support your nutrition while your mouth or throat heals, before you gradually return to eating and drinking.

The team looking after you

You will be cared for by a multidisciplinary team including your surgeons, anaesthetists, specialist nurses, speech and language therapists, dietitians, and physiotherapists, who work together to support your recovery, communication and nutrition throughout your stay.

4. What to expect afterwards

Immediately afterwards

  • Most patients spend the first one to two nights in a high-dependency or intensive care unit for close monitoring, particularly if a free flap reconstruction has been performed — the flap's blood supply is checked very closely, usually hourly, for the first few days, since good blood flow is essential for it to survive.
  • If a tracheostomy has been placed, it is cared for regularly by the nursing team and removed as soon as it is no longer needed.
  • If a feeding tube has been placed, it supports your nutrition while a speech and language therapist guides your gradual return to swallowing and eating.
  • You will then move to the ward for ongoing recovery, typically for a further one to two weeks, though this varies considerably depending on the extent of your surgery and your general health.

As you recover

  • Swelling and bruising in the operated area, the neck, and any donor site are normal in the first two to three weeks.
  • Speech and swallowing often improve steadily over weeks to months, supported by a speech and language therapist; depending on how much tissue was removed and reconstructed, some patients notice permanent changes in speech clarity or swallowing.
  • Return to a normal diet is usually gradual, starting with soft or pureed foods and building up as healing progresses and swallowing is reassessed.
  • Many patients are ready for light activities within four to six weeks, and fuller activity by two to three months, though this varies with the extent of surgery and your role — your team will advise on timing for your specific circumstances.
  • A follow-up appointment is arranged to review your progress, discuss the final pathology results, and plan any further treatment such as radiotherapy if needed.

5. Possible risks and complications

As with any major surgery, there are risks, but your surgical team monitors closely for these and has clear plans in place to manage them if they occur. Your surgeon will discuss those most relevant to your specific combination of procedures, but general categories include:

  • General surgical risks — bleeding or bruising, infection of the wound or chest, blood clots in the leg (DVT) or lung (pulmonary embolism), risks associated with a general anaesthetic, and scarring.
  • Risks specific to tumour resection — changes to speech, chewing or swallowing depending on how much tissue is removed, and numbness or altered sensation in the affected area.
  • Risks specific to neck dissection — numbness of the neck, ear or shoulder skin, and shoulder stiffness or weakness (see the separate Neck Dissection leaflet for detail).
  • Risks specific to reconstruction — a small proportion of free flaps (approximately 4% in Mr Maciver's own outcomes data) lose their blood supply and do not survive, which may require an urgent return to theatre or use of an alternative reconstruction; problems can also occur at the donor site, such as delayed healing, altered sensation, or temporary changes in strength or movement.
  • Risks specific to a tracheostomy or feeding tube, where used — these are generally well tolerated and removed once no longer needed, though local irritation or, rarely, more significant airway or tube-related complications can occur.
  • Different pathology or margin findings than expected on final examination — occasionally the final pathology shows more extensive disease, or margins that are not completely clear, than expected. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan — which may include further surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy — and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

Questions patients often ask

Will I need a tracheostomy or feeding tube?

Many, but not all, patients having combined major head and neck surgery need a temporary tracheostomy and/or feeding tube for the first days after surgery. Both are usually removed once swelling has settled and swallowing is safe. Your surgeon will tell you whether either is planned for your specific operation.

How long will I be in hospital?

Typically one to two weeks in total, including an initial period of closer monitoring in a high-dependency or intensive care unit, though this varies considerably depending on the extent of your surgery and your recovery.

Will I need radiotherapy or chemotherapy as well as surgery?

This decision is based on your final pathology results, including the size of what was removed, any spread to lymph nodes, and whether the margins are fully clear. Not everyone needs further treatment; your case will be discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, and the recommended plan will then be explained to you in person, usually once results are available around one to two weeks after surgery.

How will the surgery affect my appearance?

Reconstruction is planned to support your facial or neck shape and contour as closely as possible, but some change in appearance, symmetry, swelling, or sensation should be expected. Your team can discuss realistic expectations for your specific operation.

When can I go back to work and normal activities?

This varies with the extent of surgery and the physical demands of your role, but many patients are ready for light activities within four to six weeks and fuller activity by two to three months. Your team will advise on timing for your specific circumstances.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, breathing difficulty, or any change in the colour or warmth of a flap or wound. Use the contact details below.
