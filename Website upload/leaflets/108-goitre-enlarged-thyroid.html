Goitre (Enlarged Thyroid)

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains what a goitre is, how it is assessed and when treatment or surgery is needed. It is written for people seen by a surgeon and OMFS clinic team for a swelling in the front of the neck.

At a glance
  • A goitre is an enlarged thyroid gland. It is usually painless and very often harmless.
  • It may be smooth or lumpy (nodular), and the gland may be over-, under- or normally active.
  • Many goitres need only monitoring.
  • Surgery is considered for pressure symptoms, a goitre going behind the breastbone, an overactive gland, concern about cancer or appearance.
  • Breathing difficulty, rapid swelling or a hoarse voice need prompt assessment.

1. What is a goitre and why does it happen?

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland at the front of the neck, below the voice box. A goitre is a swelling of the whole gland, or of parts of it. It is common, particularly in women.

Causes include:

  • Not enough iodine in the diet. This is the commonest cause worldwide.
  • Nodules. Multinodular goitre develops slowly over many years, often running in families.
  • Graves' disease, an autoimmune condition that makes the gland overactive. See our leaflet 'Overactive Thyroid (Graves' Disease)'.
  • Hashimoto's thyroiditis, an autoimmune condition that tends to make the gland underactive.
  • Inflammation of the thyroid, pregnancy, and some medicines (for example lithium).
  • Rarely, a cancer of the thyroid. See our leaflet on neck lumps and thyroid swelling.

Some goitres grow downwards behind the breastbone (retrosternal or substernal goitre).

2. How is it assessed?

  • Your surgeon asks about symptoms and examines your neck.
  • Blood tests check thyroid function (TSH and thyroid hormones) and, if needed, thyroid antibodies, calcium and others.
  • An ultrasound scan shows the size and nature of the gland and any nodules.
  • A fine needle biopsy (a thin needle sample, guided by ultrasound) is used for suspicious nodules.
  • A CT or MRI scan may be needed for a very large goitre or one extending behind the breastbone, to see pressure on the windpipe. Your team will tell you if contrast dye is a concern for your thyroid.
  • A check of the voice cords with a flexible camera, if you have voice change or before surgery. See our leaflet on flexible laryngoscopy.
  • A thyroid isotope scan in some people with an overactive gland.

Symptoms to tell us about: a visible swelling, a feeling of tightness or something stuck in the throat, difficulty swallowing, noisy breathing or breathlessness when lying flat, a persistent cough, voice change, or symptoms of an overactive or underactive thyroid.

3. Treatment without surgery

  • Watchful waiting. If your goitre is small, not causing symptoms and the tests are reassuring, it is usually monitored with repeat checks, sometimes with repeat ultrasound.
  • Treating thyroid function. An overactive goitre is treated with tablets or radioactive iodine. An underactive gland is treated with thyroxine tablets.
  • Iodine. If the cause is iodine deficiency, diet advice may help. Do not take iodine or kelp supplements without advice, as too much iodine can also cause problems.
  • Radioactive iodine can shrink some goitres, particularly if overactive, and is useful when surgery is risky.
  • Thyroxine to shrink a nodular goitre is generally not recommended, as it is rarely helpful.

4. When is surgery recommended?

  • The goitre presses on the windpipe or gullet, causing breathing or swallowing problems.
  • It extends behind the breastbone.
  • A nodule is suspicious or proven to be a cancer.
  • The goitre is overactive and not controlled by other treatment.
  • The goitre is large or growing and the appearance bothers you.

The operation is a thyroidectomy, which removes half or all of the gland, through a neck incision under general anaesthetic. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. A small drain is occasionally used. Stitches are dissolvable. Most people go home after 1 night.

Risks are set out in our leaflet 'Consequences of Thyroid Surgery'. In short: temporary voice change in about 1 in 20 (3 to 8%) and permanent in about 1 in 100; low calcium, which affects about 1 in 4 people briefly after removal of the whole gland and is usually temporary, with about 1 in 14 still needing tablets at six months; bleeding needing a return to theatre in about 1 in 80; bleeding, infection and a scar. Surgery for a large or retrosternal goitre carries a somewhat higher chance of nerve and calcium problems. After a total thyroidectomy you need thyroxine for life, and about 1 in 4 to 1 in 3 people need it after removal of half the gland.

Occasionally the removed tissue is found, when examined under the microscope, to contain a cancer. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

5. Expected outcome

Most goitres are benign (not cancer) and can be monitored safely. After surgery, pressure symptoms such as difficulty swallowing and noisy breathing usually improve. A goitre can come back if part of the thyroid is left behind. Your surgeon will talk to you about the right amount to remove.

Questions patients often ask

Is a goitre cancer?

Usually not. Cancer is found in a small minority of nodules, which is why tests are done.

Does a goitre mean my thyroid is not working?

Not necessarily. A goitre can be over-, under- or normally active. Blood tests tell us.

Can diet make a goitre go away?

Only if it is caused by lack of iodine. Please ask before taking supplements.

When should I seek urgent help?

If you develop difficulty breathing, noisy breathing, rapid swelling of the neck or difficulty swallowing.
