Open Jaw Joint Surgery (Open TMJ Surgery)

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains open surgery on the jaw joint, why it is done and what to expect. Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team will talk it through with you.

At a glance
  • Open TMJ surgery uses a cut in front of the ear so your surgeon can see and treat the jaw joint directly.
  • It is done under general anaesthetic and usually takes about an hour for each joint.
  • Your surgeon may reposition, repair, reshape or remove the cartilage disc.
  • You should go home the same day or the day after.
  • Most people return to work after a few days to a couple of weeks. Jaw exercises are an important part of recovery.

1. Why has this operation been recommended?

The jaw joint, or temporomandibular joint (TMJ), sits just in front of the ear. Open surgery lets your surgeon see inside the joint and treat problems such as a torn or displaced cartilage disc, or damage to the joint surfaces, that cannot be treated by simpler means. If your jaw keeps coming out of joint (dislocating), open surgery may also be needed.

The aim is to confirm exactly what the problem is and treat it at the same time.

Alternatives

Many jaw joint problems settle without an operation. Your surgeon will usually start with simple measures such as a soft diet and rest for the jaw, anti-inflammatory painkillers, treatment of teeth grinding (bruxism) including a soft splint made by your dentist, and sometimes anti-inflammatory gel, muscle-relaxing medicines, jaw exercises, Botox or a steroid injection into the joint. Scans (CT or MRI) are often used to look at the joint without surgery.

Open surgery is usually only offered when these, and often arthrocentesis or arthroscopy, have not helped enough.

2. The anaesthetic

The operation is done under general anaesthetic, so you will be fully asleep. You must not drive or operate machinery for 48 hours afterwards.

Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

3. What does the operation involve?

Your surgeon makes a cut in front of your ear so the joint can be opened and examined. It usually takes about an hour for each joint. What is done depends on what is found:

  • Disc out of place. The disc is held in position with stitches, or with a small metal pin in the bone.
  • Disc damaged. The disc is removed. It may be replaced with tissue from another part of your body.
  • Joint surface damaged. The surface may be reshaped to make it smoother.
  • Jaw keeps coming out of joint. The upper joint surface may be altered or removed.

The cut is then closed with stitches, usually dissolvable, and a dressing is applied. If tissue is taken from elsewhere, you will have a second small wound there. Your surgeon will explain this before the operation.

4. What to expect afterwards

  • Swelling and discomfort. These are usually worst at about two days. Painkillers will be arranged.
  • Going home. You should be able to go home the same day or the day after.
  • Work. You should be able to return to work after a few days, depending on your job.
  • Eating. A soft diet is more comfortable at first. Avoid chewing gum and very hard or chewy food as advised by your surgeon.
  • Jaw exercises. These are a very important part of recovery. They help to restore movement and reduce stiffness and long-term soreness. Please see our leaflet on jaw exercises after TMJ surgery.
  • Review. A follow-up appointment will be arranged, usually about three weeks after surgery.

5. Possible complications

Published percentages vary with the operation done, so the risks are described in words. Your surgeon will tell you your own risks.

General

  • Pain.
  • Bleeding.
  • Bruising and swelling of the face.
  • Infection of the wound.
  • Blood clots in the legs (rare).

Specific to the jaw joint

  • Not being able to open the mouth fully (trismus) and jaw stiffness, which is why exercises matter.
  • Tenderness and pain in the joint, which may continue.
  • Numbness of the temple and ear. This is common near the cut and may be lasting.
  • Weak forehead movement. This is due to a branch of the facial nerve near the joint and is usually temporary, but it can occasionally be longer-lasting.
  • A change in your bite.
  • The scar in front of the ear. It usually fades well.
  • The problem not improving, or coming back, and further surgery.

Questions patients often ask

Will I have a scar?

Yes, a fine scar in front of the ear that usually fades well and is hidden by the natural fold.

Will my jaw be wired?

No.

How soon can I return to normal activities?

Many people return to work after a few days. Avoid strenuous exercise until your surgeon agrees.

What if the surgery does not fully work?

Further treatment is available. Your surgeon will discuss this with you.
