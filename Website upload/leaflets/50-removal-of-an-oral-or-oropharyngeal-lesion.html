Removal of an Oral or Oropharyngeal Lesion

Surgery to remove a lump, patch, or ulcer from the mouth or throat

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has surgery been recommended?

A lesion — a lump, red or white patch, or ulcer — within the mouth (oral cavity) or the part of the throat just behind it (the oropharynx) is usually removed for one of two closely related reasons: to establish a definite diagnosis where this cannot be reached by biopsy alone, or as treatment once a diagnosis is already known, removing the whole lesion together with a margin of healthy tissue around it.

This uncertainty is exactly why surgical removal is usually recommended: taking out the whole lesion allows a specialist pathologist to examine it fully and tell you precisely what it is, giving you and your surgical team a clear, reliable diagnosis on which to base any further care.

2. The anaesthetic

Smaller lesions can sometimes be removed under local anaesthetic alone, but this operation is more commonly carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. Your surgeon will tell you which is planned for you. If a general anaesthetic is planned, you will have an anaesthetic assessment before your operation to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable.

3. What does the procedure involve?

  • The lesion is removed together with a margin of surrounding healthy tissue, to give the best chance of complete removal and an accurate diagnosis.
  • For smaller defects, the resulting gap is closed directly with dissolvable stitches.
  • For larger defects, the area may instead be left to heal on its own (by 'secondary intention'), covered with a small skin graft, or, occasionally, reconstructed with a local flap of nearby tissue. Your surgeon will explain which approach is planned for your specific lesion.
  • The removed tissue is sent to a specialist pathologist for detailed examination.

4. What to expect afterwards

Immediately afterwards

  • Many patients go home the same day or after one night in hospital, depending on the size of the lesion and the type of anaesthetic used.
  • Some swelling, discomfort, and difficulty eating or speaking are normal for the first few days while the area heals.
  • You will be advised on diet (often a soft diet initially) and mouth care for the first week or two.

As you recover

  • Discomfort and swelling generally settle within one to two weeks, and the stitches dissolve on their own.
  • Your pathology results are usually available around one to two weeks after surgery, and a follow-up appointment is arranged to discuss them with you.
  • Full healing of the area, including any graft or flap used, typically continues over several weeks.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications are possible, but the great majority of patients recover well from this operation. Your surgeon will discuss those most relevant to your specific lesion, but general categories include:

  • Pain, swelling, and bleeding — expected to some degree, and settling over one to two weeks.
  • Difficulty or discomfort swallowing (dysphagia) — common in the first few days while the area heals, and usually settling as swelling resolves.
  • An abnormal opening between the mouth and the sinus or nasal cavity (an oroantral or oronasal fistula) — an uncommon complication, more relevant to lesions near the upper jaw or palate, and usually manageable if it occurs.
  • The biopsy may not give a full or definite diagnosis, occasionally requiring a further biopsy or procedure.
  • Further surgery or further treatment may occasionally be needed, depending on the final pathology results.

Occasionally a lump turns out to be a cancer once it has been fully examined under the microscope. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

Your team monitors closely for these complications and has clear plans in place to manage them. Please raise any worries with them — they would rather you ask.

Questions patients often ask

Will I know straight away what the lesion is?

Not usually. The tissue removed is sent for detailed examination by a specialist pathologist, which typically takes one to two weeks. You will be given a follow-up appointment to discuss the results in person.

Will I need a general anaesthetic?

This depends on the size and position of your lesion. Smaller ones can sometimes be removed under local anaesthetic, while larger or less accessible ones are usually removed under general anaesthetic. Your surgeon will discuss which applies to you.

Will I have a scar or a visible change inside my mouth?

Some change to the appearance and feel of the area is expected, particularly for larger lesions, but this often settles well as healing progresses. Your surgeon can discuss what to expect for your specific lesion.

Will I need further treatment?

This depends on the final pathology results. If anything unexpected is found, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting — a group of specialists who together agree the most appropriate plan — and this will then be explained to you in person by your surgeon before anything further is arranged.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, bleeding, or fever. Use the contact details below.
