Oropharyngeal Cancer (Cancer of the Middle Throat)

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains cancer of the oropharynx, the middle part of the throat. It covers all causes. If your cancer is linked to HPV, please also read our HPV-related throat cancer leaflet. This is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team.

At a glance
  • The oropharynx includes the tonsils, the back third of the tongue, the soft palate and the back wall of the throat.
  • Causes include HPV infection, smoking and alcohol.
  • A lump in the neck is often the first and sometimes the only sign.
  • Treatment is surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy or a combination. Keyhole surgery through the mouth may be possible.
  • Cancers caused by HPV generally respond better to treatment. Many people with more advanced cancers are cured.

1. What is oropharyngeal cancer and why does it happen?

The oropharynx is the middle part of the throat, directly behind the mouth. It has four parts: the sides of the throat including the tonsils; the base (back third) of the tongue; the soft palate (the soft roof of the mouth) and uvula; and the back wall of the throat. It helps you breathe and pushes food and drink down to the stomach. The base of the tongue also helps stop food and drink entering the voice box.

Cancer happens when cells become abnormal, grow in an uncontrolled way and can spread. Most oropharyngeal cancers start in the lining cells (squamous cell carcinoma). Your doctor may describe it by where it started, such as a tonsil or tongue-base cancer.

What causes it?

  • Human papillomavirus (HPV), especially type 16. HPV causes many oropharyngeal cancers. See our leaflet on HPV-related throat cancer.
  • Smoking or using chewing tobacco.
  • Heavy alcohol use over many years, especially with smoking.

Possible symptoms

  • A sore throat or ulcer that does not go away.
  • A lump in the throat.
  • A lump in the neck. This is common and may be the only sign.
  • Difficulty swallowing or moving the tongue, or opening the mouth.
  • Ear pain, voice change, noisy breathing, coughing up blood or unexplained weight loss.

Most of these have other causes, but if any last more than a few weeks, please have them checked.

2. How is it diagnosed?

Your surgeon will ask about your symptoms, health, smoking and alcohol use, and examine your mouth, throat and neck. Not everyone needs every test.

  • Nasoendoscopy: a thin flexible camera passed through the nose to see the throat, done in clinic.
  • Biopsy: a sample from the throat or a neck lump. If the area cannot be reached safely in clinic, a short general anaesthetic is used, often as a day case. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.
  • Needle biopsy (FNA) or core biopsy of a neck lump, guided by ultrasound.
  • CT, MRI and PET-CT scans show the size of the cancer and whether it has spread.
  • Testing for HPV on the biopsy, because it affects the outlook and sometimes the treatment.

Staging

Staging describes the size of the cancer and whether it has spread (T, N and M). The cause matters too. HPV-related oropharyngeal cancers respond better to treatment than similar cancers caused by smoking, so the same stage can mean a different outlook. Many people with advanced stages are cured.

3. Your specialist team

Your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

The team includes head and neck surgeons, reconstructive surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, oncologists (cancer doctors who give radiotherapy and chemotherapy), specialist nurses, speech and swallowing therapists, dietitians, dental and oral medicine specialists, and psychological and social support. You are always welcome to ask for a second opinion.

4. Treatment options

The best treatment depends on the size and position of the cancer, whether it has spread, whether it is linked to HPV or smoking, your general health and your wishes. The options are surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, often combined. For many oropharyngeal cancers, radiotherapy (with chemotherapy for larger cancers) and surgery give similar chances of cure, so your preferences matter.

Surgery

Surgery is done under general anaesthetic. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

  • Transoral robotic or laser surgery (TORS): the cancer is removed through the open mouth without any cuts on the outside. It is suitable for some smaller cancers.
  • Neck dissection: removal of lymph nodes from one or both sides of the neck, even if scans show no cancer in them, because tiny deposits can be missed. It is often done with TORS.
  • Mandibulotomy: for larger or further-back cancers, the jaw bone is divided to reach the cancer and then fixed back with small titanium plates.
  • Free flap reconstruction: if a larger area of tissue is removed, skin and muscle (and sometimes bone) with their blood supply are taken from another part of the body and used to rebuild the area. In our surgeon's own experience about 4 in 100 flaps (around 4%) do not survive and need further surgery.
  • Temporary tracheostomy: a breathing tube placed in the windpipe through a small opening in the lower neck, used only after bigger operations while swelling settles. It is usually removed within about a week once normal breathing is safe.

A small drain is occasionally used and stitches are dissolvable. Your surgeon will tell you how long you will stay in hospital, which depends on the operation. A feeding tube through the nose is often used for a time after larger operations.

Radiotherapy and chemotherapy

  • Radiotherapy alone or with chemotherapy (chemoradiotherapy) given as the main treatment, daily on weekdays for about 7 weeks.
  • After surgery, radiotherapy (sometimes with chemotherapy) is added if the pathology report, which comes 1 to 2 weeks after surgery, shows a higher risk of the cancer returning. It usually starts about 4 weeks after surgery and lasts about 6 weeks.

Side effects of radiotherapy can include:

  • tiredness
  • sore, red or dry skin in the treated area
  • a sore mouth and throat, and pain on swallowing
  • dry mouth and thickened saliva
  • changes in taste, and a loss of appetite and weight
  • a hoarse voice

Most side effects start around two weeks into treatment, are at their worst just after it finishes and improve over the following 4 to 6 weeks. Some can last for months and a few may be permanent, such as a dry mouth. Because radiotherapy can affect the jaw bone, any broken or infected teeth are usually treated before it starts, and regular dental check-ups afterwards are important. Speech and swallowing therapists and dietitians support you before, during and after treatment. Some people need a feeding tube for a time.

5. Follow-up and outlook

After treatment you will have regular check-ups, including examination and camera checks of the throat. Scans are used when needed. After chemoradiotherapy a PET-CT scan is often arranged around 12 weeks later to check the cancer has gone.

The outlook depends on the cause (HPV or smoking), the stage, the treatment and your general health. HPV-related cancers generally have a better outlook. Many people are cured, including many with larger cancers. Your team will talk this through with you honestly and in person.

If you smoke or drink alcohol, stopping or cutting down helps your recovery and lowers the chance of a new cancer. Ask the team for help. Support is available from your dietitian, speech and swallowing therapist, dentist, counsellor and social worker. Feeling anxious or low is very common. Please tell us if you are struggling.

Questions patients often ask

Is this the same as HPV throat cancer?

HPV causes many oropharyngeal cancers, but not all. This leaflet covers both. See the HPV leaflet if yours is HPV-related.

Do I need surgery or radiotherapy?

Often either can cure it. Your team will explain which suits you best, and the final plan is agreed with you.

Will I be able to swallow and speak normally?

Treatment can affect swallowing and speech for a while, and most people improve with help from the speech and swallowing therapist.

Can I pass HPV to my partner?

Most adults carry HPV at some point without problems. Ask your team if you are worried. Our HPV leaflet has more.
