Having a Sialogram

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains what to expect when your surgeon and OMFS clinic team arrange a sialogram, an X-ray dye test of a salivary gland.

At a glance
  • A sialogram is a dye X-ray test of a salivary gland and its duct (drainage tube).
  • It is used to look for stones, narrowing or other problems in the gland.
  • It is done by a radiologist (X-ray doctor) and takes about 30 minutes.
  • You can eat, drink and take your medicines as normal beforehand.
  • It can be uncomfortable as the dye goes in, but is usually not painful.
  • The report takes several days, and you will be sent a clinic appointment to discuss it.

1. What is a sialogram and why is it done?

A sialogram is a dye investigation of a salivary gland. It looks in detail at the larger salivary glands: the parotid gland (in front of and below the ear) and the submandibular gland (under the jaw).

These glands drain saliva into your mouth through small tubes called ducts. The parotid duct opens on the inside of the cheek next to the upper back teeth. The submandibular duct opens under the tongue, just behind the lower front teeth.

A sialogram can show stones, narrowing of the duct, or other changes that may cause pain or swelling in a gland, particularly when you eat.

2. How do I prepare?

You can eat, drink and take your usual medicines before your sialogram. Tell the team if you are or might be pregnant, if you have allergies (especially to iodine-containing dyes or X-ray contrast), and if you currently have a painful, infected gland, as the test may need to be delayed.

3. What does a sialogram involve?

  • The radiologist gently passes a very small, blunt plastic tube into the opening of the duct inside your mouth.
  • The tube is blunt, so this part is not usually painful and does not need a local anaesthetic injection.
  • Dye is slowly injected through the tube into the gland. This takes a few minutes.
  • The tube is removed and X-ray pictures are taken.
  • You may be asked to suck a sour sweet or lemon taste afterwards to help the dye drain, and have a few more pictures.

It usually takes around 30 minutes from start to finish. A longer test does not mean that something is wrong.

4. What to expect afterwards

A sialogram is painless, although you may feel some pressure, fullness or discomfort in the gland as the dye is injected. Afterwards the gland may feel a little full or tender for a few hours, and you may taste the dye. This settles quickly.

You can usually eat, drink and return to normal activities straight away. Simple painkillers such as paracetamol are usually enough if needed.

5. Possible problems

Sialography is generally safe, and problems are uncommon.

  • Pain or swelling of the gland after the test: uncommon and usually settles in a day or two.
  • Infection of the gland: uncommon. Tell us if you develop increasing pain, swelling, a fever or pus in the mouth.
  • Allergic reaction to the dye: rare.
  • Small injury to the duct: rare.

Radiation from the test is low.

6. Results

It takes a long time to study all the pictures, so the report will not be available for several days. You will be sent an appointment to see your surgeon in the outpatient clinic to discuss the findings and any further treatment that might be needed.

Sometimes the pictures do not give a complete answer. Your surgeon will explain what was seen and how this helps plan the right care for you.

Questions patients often ask

Is it painful?

It is not usually painful, but you may feel discomfort as the dye is injected.

Will I need an anaesthetic?

No. The tube is blunt and no injection is usually needed.

Is there an alternative?

Ultrasound, MRI sialography and sialendoscopy (a tiny telescope inside the duct) can also be used. Your surgeon will explain which test suits you best.
