Thyroid Lump or Nodule: Investigation and Surgery

Understanding thyroid nodules, how they are assessed, and what to expect from hemithyroidectomy or total thyroidectomy

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. Why has surgery been recommended?

Your thyroid gland sits in the lower front of your neck, in front of the windpipe (trachea) and just below the voice box (larynx). It produces hormones that regulate your metabolism. Lumps or swellings (nodules) within it are very common, and the great majority are benign — often simple cysts or benign overgrowths of thyroid tissue. Occasionally a nodule proves to be a thyroid cancer, which is why any new or growing nodule is properly assessed before deciding on treatment.

How a thyroid nodule is assessed

An ultrasound scan looks at the size, shape and internal features of the nodule to judge how likely it is to be significant. If features suggest this is worthwhile, a fine needle aspiration (FNA) — a thin needle passed into the nodule to collect cells for examination under the microscope — is usually performed, often on the same visit.

FNA results are reported using a standard scoring system (the Bethesda System, with an equivalent UK Thy classification), which groups the sample into one of six categories according to how confidently it can be read and what it shows. Each category carries an approximate risk of the nodule being cancerous, shown below:

They are periodically revised, so your own result will be explained to you specifically rather than relying on the category alone. A non-diagnostic result (Bethesda 1) usually means the sample didn't contain enough cells to read confidently, and the FNA is often simply repeated rather than indicating anything abnormal.

Which operation is recommended?

There are two main operations, and which one is recommended depends on your ultrasound and FNA findings, the size and position of the nodule, and whether one or both sides of the gland are involved. A hemithyroidectomy removes only the lobe (one side) containing the nodule, leaving the other side to continue producing thyroid hormone — this is often suitable for a nodule confined to one side, particularly where the FNA result is indeterminate (Bethesda 3 or 4) and surgery is needed mainly to obtain a definite diagnosis. A total thyroidectomy removes the whole gland, and is usually recommended when a cancer is confirmed or strongly suspected, when nodules affect both sides, or for a large goitre causing symptoms. Your surgeon will explain which operation applies to you and why.

Scans and needle tests cannot always be completely certain about what a nodule is. Removing the nodule, or the lobe containing it, is often the only way to give you a definite answer, and that is the reason surgery is being recommended.

2. The anaesthetic

This operation is carried out under general anaesthetic, meaning you will be fully asleep throughout. A hemithyroidectomy typically takes around 60 to 90 minutes; a total thyroidectomy typically takes around 90 minutes to two and a half hours, depending on the size of the gland and any previous inflammation or scarring. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic. Your anaesthetist will be with you throughout the operation to keep you safe and comfortable.

3. What does the procedure involve?

Protecting the nerves and parathyroid glands

Two structures lying very close to the thyroid are carefully identified and protected throughout: the recurrent laryngeal nerves, which control the voice box and are essential for a normal voice, and the parathyroid glands — four tiny glands, usually two on each side, which control your blood calcium level. A nerve monitor and surgical loupes (magnifying eyewear) are used routinely to help identify and protect the recurrent laryngeal nerve on the side(s) being operated on, with the monitor confirming the nerve is working at each stage.

The procedure

  • An incision is made in a natural low crease in the front of the neck, positioned to keep the resulting scar as inconspicuous as possible.
  • A flap is raised beneath the neck muscles, and the strap muscles overlying the thyroid are divided in the midline to expose the gland.
  • The thyroid lobe is carefully dissected away from its blood supply and surrounding tissue, working close to the gland's own capsule to protect nearby structures.
  • The parathyroid glands and recurrent laryngeal nerve on that side are identified and preserved throughout, with the nerve monitor confirming the nerve is working as the dissection proceeds.
  • For a hemithyroidectomy, the affected lobe is freed and removed, and the operation ends there. For a total thyroidectomy, the same steps are then repeated on the opposite side so that the whole gland is removed.
  • The recurrent laryngeal nerve is tested with the nerve stimulator at the end of the dissection to confirm it is working well.
  • The wound is closed in layers with dissolvable stitches, and Steri-Strips or skin glue are applied. A small drain is sometimes left beneath the skin, particularly after a total thyroidectomy, and is removed after a day or so once drainage has settled.

4. What to expect afterwards

Immediately afterwards

  • Most patients stay in hospital overnight, though a hemithyroidectomy can sometimes be done as a day case.
  • After a total thyroidectomy, your blood calcium level is checked around six hours after surgery and again every six hours for the first 24 hours, since the parathyroid glands can be temporarily 'stunned' by the operation even when carefully preserved.
  • You will be given antibiotics intravenously for the first 24 hours and regular pain relief.
  • If a drain has been used, it is usually removed after a day or so once drainage has settled.
  • Some swelling, bruising and tightness in the neck, and mild throat discomfort or a slightly husky voice, are normal in the first few days.

As you recover

  • Swelling and bruising generally settle over one to two weeks, and the scar continues to fade over several months.
  • Most people manage light activities within a week or two, with a fuller return to normal activity, including work, over two to three weeks.
  • After a total thyroidectomy, you will need to take levothyroxine (thyroid hormone replacement) every day for life, usually starting the day after surgery, with blood tests to fine-tune the dose over the following weeks and months.
  • After a hemithyroidectomy, the remaining lobe often produces enough hormone on its own, but around 1 in 4 patients eventually need levothyroxine too — this is checked with a blood test at your follow-up appointment and periodically afterwards.
  • A follow-up appointment is arranged to give you the final pathology results and discuss whether any further treatment is needed.
  • If the final pathology confirms a cancer, your case will be discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, and any recommended further treatment — which may include radioactive iodine or, occasionally, completing removal of the remaining lobe — will be explained to you in person.

5. Possible risks and complications

Complications of thyroid surgery are possible, but the great majority of patients recover without experiencing any of them, and most of those that do occur are manageable and settle over time. The approximate figures below are taken from published studies of large numbers of patients, so you can make an informed decision — your own surgeon will discuss which are most relevant to your individual case:

  • Bleeding or a collection of blood under the skin (a haematoma) — occurs in around 1 in 70 operations; very rarely this can press on the airway and needs urgent return to theatre, which is why you are watched closely in the hours after surgery.
  • Temporary voice change or hoarseness, from handling of the recurrent laryngeal nerve — affects roughly 1 in 10 patients (about 5 to 15 in 100) and usually recovers over weeks to a few months. Permanent voice change is much less common, affecting around 1 in 100 patients.
  • Temporary low blood calcium (hypocalcaemia), from the parathyroid glands being 'stunned' by surgery — common after a total thyroidectomy, affecting roughly 1 in 4 patients, and usually managed with calcium and vitamin D supplements until the glands recover over several weeks. Permanent low calcium needing lifelong supplements is uncommon, affecting around 1 in 30 patients.
  • Need for lifelong thyroid hormone (levothyroxine) — certain after a total thyroidectomy, since no thyroid tissue remains; after a hemithyroidectomy this affects around 1 in 4 patients, depending on how well the remaining lobe functions.
  • Wound infection — uncommon and usually settles with antibiotics.
  • Different pathology than expected on final examination — occasionally a nodule turns out to be a cancer once it has been fully examined under the microscope, even when it looked benign beforehand. If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

Questions patients often ask

Will I definitely need thyroid hormone tablets afterwards?

After a total thyroidectomy, yes — no thyroid tissue remains, so lifelong levothyroxine is needed, with blood tests to get the dose right. After a hemithyroidectomy, most people don't need it, but around 1 in 4 eventually do if the remaining lobe doesn't quite keep up, which is checked with routine blood tests.

Will my voice be affected?

A temporary huskiness or voice change affects roughly 1 in 10 patients and usually settles over weeks to a few months, as the nerve controlling the voice box is handled carefully throughout surgery and monitored with a nerve monitor. Permanent voice change is uncommon, affecting around 1 in 100 patients.

How is it decided whether I need one side removed or the whole gland?

This depends on your ultrasound and FNA findings, whether one or both sides are involved, and whether cancer is confirmed or strongly suspected. Your surgeon will explain the reasoning behind the recommendation for your specific case.

What happens if the lump turns out to be a cancer?

If this happens, your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting — a group of specialists who together agree the most appropriate plan — and this will then be explained to you in person by your surgeon. Further treatment, if needed, may include radioactive iodine treatment or, occasionally, a second operation to remove any remaining thyroid tissue.

Will I need radioactive iodine treatment?

This depends on the type and stage of any cancer found and is decided by the multidisciplinary team. Many low-risk thyroid cancers need no further treatment beyond surgery and regular follow-up; others benefit from radioactive iodine afterwards to treat any remaining thyroid cells.

How long will I be in hospital?

Most patients stay one night; a hemithyroidectomy can sometimes be done as a day case depending on your recovery and blood calcium results.

Will I have a visible scar?

The incision is placed in a natural low neck crease specifically to keep it as inconspicuous as possible, and it typically fades well over the following months.

When can I go back to work and normal activities?

Many patients manage light activities within one to two weeks and fuller activity, including work, within two to three weeks.

Who can I contact if I have concerns after I go home?

Please get in touch promptly if you notice increasing pain, swelling, redness, fever, bleeding, difficulty breathing, or tingling around your lips or fingertips (a possible sign of low calcium). Use the contact details below.
