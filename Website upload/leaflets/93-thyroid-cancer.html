Thyroid Cancer

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains what thyroid cancer is, how it is diagnosed and how it is treated. It is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team. Your own plan will be agreed with you in person.

At a glance
  • Thyroid cancer usually starts as a painless lump in the front of the neck. Most thyroid lumps are not cancer.
  • The most common types (papillary and follicular) grow slowly and have a very high chance of cure.
  • Diagnosis uses an ultrasound scan, a needle test, a voice-box check and blood tests.
  • Surgery is the main treatment. Some people also need radioactive iodine, and most need thyroid hormone tablets afterwards.
  • Your plan is agreed by a specialist head and neck team (the MDT).

1. What is thyroid cancer and why does it happen?

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland at the base of the neck, in front of the windpipe and just below the voice box. It makes hormones that control how fast your body uses energy. A lump in the thyroid is called a nodule. Thyroid nodules are very common and most are not cancer. For lumps in the neck and thyroid in general, please see our leaflet on neck lumps and thyroid swelling.

Cancer happens when cells become abnormal, grow in an uncontrolled way and can spread. Thyroid cancer is uncommon and is two to three times more common in women than in men.

The main types

  • Papillary: the most common (about 3 in 4 thyroid cancers). It grows slowly and often spreads to lymph nodes in the neck, but is usually cured or controlled.
  • Follicular: about 1 in 20 to 1 in 10 thyroid cancers. It grows slowly and usually stays in the thyroid. If found late it can spread to the lungs or bones.
  • Medullary: rare. It starts from different cells that make a hormone called calcitonin. Some are inherited (see below).
  • Anaplastic: very rare (under 1 in 100). It grows quickly and needs urgent specialist care.
  • Lymphoma of the thyroid: a cancer of immune system cells. It is treated differently and is usually managed with the cancer doctors (oncologists).

What causes it?

In most people we do not know the cause. Factors that can raise the risk include previous radiotherapy or radiation exposure to the head and neck, a history of thyroid nodules or goitre (an enlarged thyroid), and a family history. Medullary cancer can be caused by a change in a gene called RET. If you have this, close family members can be offered a simple test. Weight and general health may play a small part.

Possible symptoms

  • A painless lump or swelling in the front of the neck, or a lump at the side of the neck (a lymph node).
  • A voice that is hoarse for more than a few weeks.
  • Less often, difficulty swallowing, difficulty breathing or a feeling of pressure in the neck.

Many thyroid cancers cause no symptoms and are found by chance on a scan done for another reason. Thyroid cancer rarely changes the level of your thyroid hormones. If a lump or voice change lasts more than a few weeks, please have it checked.

2. How is it diagnosed?

Your surgeon will ask about your symptoms, examine your neck and check your voice. Tests are chosen for you, and not everyone needs every test.

  • Blood tests: thyroid hormones, and sometimes calcitonin if a medullary cancer is possible.
  • Ultrasound scan of the neck: shows the lump and the nearby lymph nodes.
  • Fine needle aspiration (FNA): a thin needle takes cells from the lump, guided by ultrasound. It feels like a pinch and can be uncomfortable for a moment.
  • Nasoendoscopy or laryngoscopy: a thin flexible camera passed through the nose to see how well your vocal cords move, because the nerve to the voice box runs behind the thyroid.
  • CT or MRI scan: sometimes used to show how far a larger cancer has spread. PET scan: used only in some cases.

A needle test cannot always give a certain answer. Sometimes the result is 'uncertain' and the final answer comes only after part of the thyroid has been removed and examined under the microscope by a pathologist. If the needle test is uncertain, an operation to remove half of the thyroid may be advised both to find out and to treat.

Staging

Staging describes how big the cancer is and whether it has spread to lymph nodes or elsewhere. Thyroid cancer staging is different from most other cancers, because it depends on your age. The stage alone does not always predict your outcome, because many people with a higher stage of papillary or follicular cancer are still cured. Your team will explain what your stage means for you.

3. Your specialist team

Your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any further treatment goes ahead.

The team includes head and neck surgeons, reconstructive surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, oncologists (cancer doctors who give radiotherapy and chemotherapy), specialist nurses, speech and swallowing therapists, dietitians, dental and oral medicine specialists, and psychological and social support. You are always welcome to ask for a second opinion.

4. Treatment options

The best treatment depends on the type and size of the cancer, whether it has spread, your general health and your wishes.

Surgery

Surgery is the main treatment. It is done under general anaesthetic. Before your operation, you will have an anaesthetic assessment to check your fitness for a general anaesthetic — this is also your opportunity to discuss any specific questions or concerns about the anaesthetic.

  • Hemithyroidectomy (lobectomy): removal of half of the thyroid. Suitable for smaller, lower-risk cancers.
  • Total thyroidectomy: removal of the whole gland. Used for larger cancers, cancers in both sides, or when radioactive iodine treatment is planned.
  • Neck dissection: removal of lymph nodes from around the thyroid or the side of the neck, if cancer has spread to them or is likely to have done so.
  • Completion thyroidectomy: a second operation to remove the rest of the thyroid, if the final pathology shows it is needed.

Many people go home the same day or the next day. A small drain is occasionally used. Stitches are dissolvable and placed under the skin. The scar sits in a natural neck crease and fades over many months.

Risks of thyroid surgery

  • Temporary hoarseness (injury to the nerve to the voice box): about 5 in 100 people (5%; published figures range from about 2 to 10%, a little higher after half-thyroid surgery or repeat surgery). Most recover within weeks, and nearly all within 6 to 12 months.
  • Permanent voice change: about 1 in 100 (1%; published figures are 0 to 2%).
  • Low calcium (the parathyroid glands sit next to the thyroid): after half a thyroid it is unlikely to cause lasting problems. After a total thyroidectomy it is more common, affecting about 1 in 4 people (25%) in the first few days, with tingling around the mouth or in the fingers. It is treated with calcium and vitamin D and usually settles, but about 3 to 7 in 100 people (3 to 7%) are still on calcium or vitamin D tablets six months later.
  • Underactive thyroid: after removing half of the gland, about 1 in 4 people (23%; published range 19 to 27%) eventually need thyroxine tablets. After a total thyroidectomy everyone needs thyroxine for life.
  • Bleeding or a blood clot in the neck, infection and scarring. A blood clot is uncommon but is a reason to call us straight away if your neck swells quickly.
  • If a neck dissection is done, there can be numbness, and occasionally shoulder stiffness or weakness.

Your surgeon will go through the risks that apply to you. These figures are for thyroid surgery in general.

Thyroid hormone tablets (thyroxine)

You take one tablet a day, at the same time each day, usually first thing in the morning on an empty stomach. Blood tests are used to adjust the dose. If you have had a higher-risk cancer, a slightly higher dose may be used on purpose to lower a hormone called TSH. TSH can encourage thyroid cancer cells to grow. Please do not change your dose or stop the tablets without talking to your team. Tell us if you become pregnant, because the dose may need to be raised.

Radioactive iodine

Thyroid cells and papillary or follicular cancer cells take up iodine. A radioactive iodine capsule or liquid can therefore destroy any thyroid cells left after surgery. It is usually given at least 4 to 5 weeks after surgery and is not suitable in pregnancy or breastfeeding. You will have a low-iodine diet for about 2 weeks beforehand, and either stop thyroxine for a time or have Thyrogen injections to raise TSH. You may stay in hospital for 1 to 3 days and follow simple safety rules at home for a short time, such as keeping a distance from children and pregnant women. A scan afterwards checks for any remaining cells. Not everyone needs this. Your MDT will decide based on your final pathology.

Other treatments

External radiotherapy is rarely used and is mainly for cancers that cannot be fully removed. Chemotherapy is not usual for thyroid cancer. For advanced cancer that no longer responds to other treatments, newer targeted tablets may be offered by the cancer doctors. Because thyroid cancer is rare, there are few clinical trials, but you may be offered one if a suitable trial exists.

If a medullary or anaplastic cancer is found

These types are treated differently and more urgently. A medullary cancer usually needs a total thyroidectomy and neck dissection, and genetic advice for the family. An anaplastic cancer needs rapid assessment by the full team. This is explained to you in person.

5. Follow-up and outlook

After treatment you will have regular check-ups. These include questions about your symptoms, examination of your neck, blood tests (thyroid hormones, TSH and, for papillary and follicular cancer, thyroglobulin), and sometimes ultrasound or other scans. Follow-up usually continues for at least 10 years and often for life, because thyroid cancer can very occasionally come back many years later.

The outlook for papillary and follicular thyroid cancer is very good. In England, about 9 in 10 people with thyroid cancer (85 to 90%) are alive five years after diagnosis, and about 85 in 100 are alive after ten years, counting all types including the rare aggressive ones. For papillary cancer, the most common type, the figure is higher (about 85 to 95 in 100 at five years), and for low-risk papillary cancer about 98 in 100 people are alive from the cancer 10 to 20 years later. Medullary cancer can often be cured when found early, and anaplastic cancer is much harder to treat. Your own outlook depends on your type, stage and the final pathology. Your team will talk this through honestly with you.

A cancer diagnosis can affect people in many ways. Support is available from your dietitian, speech and swallowing therapist, dentist, counsellor and social worker. Feeling anxious or low is very common. Please tell us if you are struggling.

Questions patients often ask

Does a thyroid lump mean I have cancer?

No. Most thyroid nodules are not cancer. Every lump should be checked.

Can I live normally without a thyroid?

Yes. Thyroxine tablets replace the hormones, and most people feel well once the dose is right.

Will my voice change?

Most people have a normal voice. Temporary hoarseness is possible, and a lasting change is uncommon (about 1 in 100).

Will I need radioactive iodine?

Not always. It depends on the final pathology. The MDT will advise after your operation.

Can my family be affected?

Most thyroid cancers do not run in families. Medullary cancer sometimes does, and your team will arrange genetic advice if needed.
