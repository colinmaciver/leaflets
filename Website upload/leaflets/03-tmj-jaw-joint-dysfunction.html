TMJ (Jaw Joint) Dysfunction

Understanding jaw joint and jaw muscle pain, and how it is usually managed

This leaflet is a general guide. Your own surgeon and OMFS clinic team will discuss the details of your specific operation, as every case is different. Please write down any questions and bring them to your next appointment.

1. What is TMJ dysfunction, and why does it happen?

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is the hinge joint on each side of your jaw, just in front of your ear, connecting your lower jaw (mandible) to your skull. It works together with the muscles of chewing — the masseter, temporalis, and lateral pterygoid muscles — every time you talk, chew, or yawn.

TMJ dysfunction (sometimes called temporomandibular joint dysfunction syndrome, or TMD) describes pain and altered function around this joint and its surrounding muscles. It is very common, and in the great majority of patients it arises from the muscles rather than the joint itself — most often linked to clenching or grinding the teeth (bruxism), which is frequently worse at night or during periods of stress. This muscle overactivity can cause aching in the jaw, temple, or ear area, tension-type headaches, and a feeling of tightness or spasm in the muscles of mastication, sometimes with a temporary reduction in how far you can open your mouth (trismus).

Less commonly, TMJ dysfunction relates to a problem within the joint itself, such as displacement of the small cartilage disc that cushions the joint, or wear-and-tear (osteoarthritis) of the joint surfaces. Many patients have a combination of both muscle and joint factors.

2. How is it assessed?

Your surgeon will take a history of your symptoms — including how long you've had pain, what makes it worse (such as eating or waking up with it in the morning), and whether you notice clicking, locking, or grinding. They will examine your jaw joints and chewing muscles, checking how far you can open your mouth, whether your jaw moves evenly on opening, and whether your bite (occlusion) feels normal.

Imaging is not always needed. Plain X-rays, or occasionally a more detailed scan, are sometimes used if there is a specific concern about the joint structure itself, or if symptoms don't settle with initial conservative treatment. Many patients with typical muscle-related symptoms can be safely managed without any new imaging, based on the history and examination alone.

3. What can I do to help myself?

Most patients with TMJ dysfunction improve with simple, conservative measures aimed at resting the jaw and reducing muscle overactivity. Your surgeon may suggest:

  • A soft bite splint, usually arranged through your dentist or the AHS (Ambulatory Healthcare Service), worn to reduce the effects of clenching or grinding, particularly at night.
  • An anti-inflammatory gel, such as diclofenac gel, applied over the jaw joint or muscles twice daily for around 6 weeks.
  • A short course of a muscle relaxant, if muscle spasm is prominent, taken for a few nights at the start of treatment.
  • Referral to physiotherapy, which can help with pain relief, muscle relaxation, and jaw movement exercises.
  • Simple self-care: sticking to a softer diet and avoiding wide yawning, chewing gum, or nail-biting while symptoms settle; applying a warm compress to tight muscles; and being mindful of clenching or grinding during the day, especially at times of stress.

4. What if conservative measures don't help?

The majority of patients respond well to the conservative measures above and do not need anything further. If symptoms persist despite this, your surgeon may discuss additional options with you. These can include botulinum toxin (Botox) injection into an overactive masseter muscle to reduce clenching-related pain (see the separate Botox Injection for TMJ/Masseter Pain leaflet), or, less commonly, a joint-directed procedure such as arthrocentesis (washing out the joint) or arthroscopy, for the minority of patients whose symptoms are related to a problem within the joint itself rather than the surrounding muscles. These options are considered on an individual basis, and your surgeon will explain what, if anything, is appropriate for you.

5. What is the expected outcome?

  • Most patients notice a gradual improvement in pain and jaw movement over the weeks following the start of conservative treatment, as the muscles settle and the habit of clenching or grinding is addressed.
  • Some patients notice symptoms come and go over time, particularly at times of stress or increased clenching or grinding.
  • No formal follow-up appointment is usually arranged after initial conservative advice is given, but your surgeon is happy to see you again at any point if your symptoms continue or change.
  • The overall outlook for TMJ dysfunction is good, and most patients are able to manage their symptoms successfully with the simple measures described above.

Questions patients often ask

Is TMJ dysfunction serious?

It is very common and, for the great majority of patients, is a muscle-related problem that improves well with simple conservative measures. It is not usually a sign of anything more serious, though your surgeon will assess you individually to check for any less common causes.

Do I need an X-ray or scan?

Not always. Many patients with typical muscle-related symptoms can be safely assessed and managed on history and examination alone. Imaging is used selectively, for example if there's a specific concern about the joint itself or if symptoms don't settle with initial treatment.

How long will treatment take to work?

Response varies, but many patients notice improvement over several weeks with conservative treatment such as a splint, anti-inflammatory gel, and physiotherapy. A typical course of gel treatment is around 6 weeks.

Is grinding my teeth at night causing this?

Clenching or grinding the teeth (bruxism), which is often worse at night or during stressful periods, is a very common contributor to TMJ dysfunction. A soft splint worn at night is often recommended specifically to reduce the effect of this habit.

Will I need surgery?

Most patients do not. Surgery or joint-directed procedures are only considered for a minority of patients whose symptoms don't settle with conservative treatment, or where there's a specific problem within the joint itself.
