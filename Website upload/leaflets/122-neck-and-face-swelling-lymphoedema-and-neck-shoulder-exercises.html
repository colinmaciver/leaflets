Neck and Face Swelling (Lymphoedema) and Neck and Shoulder Exercises After Treatment

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains why swelling and stiffness can develop in the head, neck and shoulder after cancer treatment and what can help. It is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team.

At a glance
  • Surgery to the neck lymph nodes (neck dissection) or radiotherapy can slow the drainage of fluid and cause swelling of the face or neck (lymphoedema).
  • Swelling can be on the outside or inside the mouth and throat, where it cannot be seen.
  • Neck and shoulder stiffness or weakness is also common after neck surgery and radiotherapy.
  • Early review by a physiotherapist or lymphoedema practitioner improves the chance of recovery.
  • Tell your team about any new swelling, and seek help quickly if the area becomes red, hot or you develop a fever.

1. What is lymphoedema and why does it happen?

Your lymphatic system is a network of vessels and lymph nodes. The nodes filter germs. The vessels drain excess fluid from tissues and return it to the blood. If this drainage is damaged, fluid builds up and the tissues swell. This is called lymphoedema.

Why it happens in the head and neck

  • Lymph nodes were removed in a neck dissection.
  • Lymph nodes or vessels were affected by radiotherapy.
  • Cancer itself blocked the drainage.

It is more likely when surgery and radiotherapy are combined, or when both sides of the neck are treated. Surgeons and oncologists try to reduce the risk, for example by using sentinel node biopsy to remove fewer nodes, or by radiotherapy plans that limit the dose to the nodes.

What you might notice

  • Swelling of the eyelids, face, lips, neck or chin.
  • A feeling of heaviness or tightness in the face or neck.
  • Difficulty swallowing, speaking or, rarely, breathing, if the swelling is inside the mouth and throat.

Swelling may build up slowly over months and can be long-lasting. It can also appear in the first weeks after surgery or radiotherapy and settle.

2. Neck and shoulder stiffness

Some stiffness and tightness is normal for several weeks after surgery. Neck dissection can also affect the nerve that supplies a shoulder muscle, causing shoulder drop, pain and weakness. Radiotherapy can make the neck and shoulder tight and weaker over time. Loss of general fitness during treatment can add to this.

A physiotherapist can assess you, give exercises and advise when it is safe to progress. Please ask your team for an early referral. Starting early gives the best chance of recovery.

3. How is it assessed and treated?

Your surgeon or oncologist will examine you. Tests are sometimes needed if the cause of new swelling is not clear, since swelling can also be due to infection or the cancer returning. You may then be referred to a lymphoedema practitioner, who may be a physiotherapist, occupational therapist, speech therapist or specially trained nurse.

Treatment aims to reduce the swelling, stop it getting worse and ease tightness and difficulty swallowing. It usually has two stages: an intensive stage with frequent treatment, teaching and support, then a maintenance stage where you manage it yourself day to day.

What may be used

  • Manual lymphatic drainage. A gentle, specialised massage that moves fluid towards areas that drain well. You or your family may be taught a simple version.
  • Exercises. Gentle head, neck and shoulder movements and stretches that encourage lymph flow and keep joints mobile.
  • Compression. Special garments or bandages for the head and neck that soften and reduce swelling.
  • Taping. Lymphatic tape to guide fluid.
  • Skin care. Use a soap-free cleanser, moisturise daily, protect skin from sun and cuts, and treat any scratch or graze promptly by cleaning it and covering it with a dry dressing.
  • Posture and sleeping position. Small changes, such as sleeping with your head raised, may help drainage.
  • Other options. Specialised devices or laser treatment may suit some people, as decided by the lymphoedema practitioner.
See a doctor quickly if the swollen area becomes red, hot, painful or tender, or you have a fever. This may be an infection and needs treatment promptly.

4. Simple exercises and self-care

Your physiotherapist will tailor these to you. Do not start anything new if you have fresh wounds, drains or flaps, until your surgeon says it is safe. General principles:

  • Move your neck gently within a comfortable range: turn to each side, tilt your ear towards your shoulder, and look up and down.
  • Roll your shoulders backwards and shrug them gently.
  • Raise your arms slowly forwards and sideways as far as is comfortable. Aim to build up gradually.
  • Do short sessions several times a day rather than one long one.
  • Mild stretching tightness is expected. Stop if you feel sharp pain, dizziness or new numbness, and tell your team.
  • Stay active with walking or gentle exercise to rebuild fitness.

Massage over scars can also soften them once your wounds have healed. Your team will tell you when this is appropriate.

5. Expected outcome

Stiffness and shoulder problems often improve with regular exercise over weeks to months, although some people have lasting effects. Lymphoedema can be long-term for some people, but with the right help most can keep it under control and reduce its effect on daily life, swallowing and appearance. Your team will review you regularly.

Questions patients often ask

Will the swelling go away?

In the first weeks it often settles. Long-standing swelling can often be reduced and controlled, but may not disappear completely.

Is new swelling always lymphoedema?

No. New swelling can also come from infection or from the cancer returning, so always tell your team so it can be checked.

Can I fly or exercise?

Most people can. Gentle regular exercise is encouraged. Ask your team about anything vigorous or any specific concerns.

Why is my shoulder weak after my neck operation?

Neck surgery can irritate or injure the nerve to a shoulder muscle. A physiotherapist can help with exercises and advise on what recovery to expect.
