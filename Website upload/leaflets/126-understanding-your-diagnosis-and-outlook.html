Understanding Your Diagnosis and Outlook, Including When Cancer Cannot Be Cured

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet is for people with head and neck cancer, and those close to them, who want to understand what 'outlook' means and what care is available if a cancer cannot be cured. It may be difficult to read. You can read it at your own pace, with someone you trust, and you do not have to read it all at once. Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team will talk with you in person about your own situation.

At a glance
  • 'Prognosis' or 'outlook' is the doctors' best estimate of what may happen. No one can predict it exactly, because every person is different.
  • Your outlook depends on the type, place and stage of the cancer and on many personal factors.
  • Sometimes a cancer cannot be cured but can be controlled, occasionally for months or years. The aim is then to help you feel as well as possible for as long as possible.
  • Palliative and supportive care help with comfort, symptoms and support for you and your family. They are not only for the last days of life and can be given alongside cancer treatment.
  • You can ask your team anything, at any time, and bring someone with you.

1. What do 'diagnosis' and 'outlook' mean?

Your diagnosis is the type of cancer you have, where it is, and how far it has spread (its stage). Your outlook, or prognosis, is your doctors' best estimate of what is likely to happen. It includes the chance that treatment will cure the cancer. If the cancer cannot be cured, it can also mean how it may behave over time.

The type, place and stage of the cancer all matter, and so do your general health and how the cancer responds to treatment. Because so many things play a part, no doctor can predict the course of an illness exactly. General statistics describe groups of people, not you as an individual. For this reason we do not quote numbers in this leaflet. If you would like to hear them, please ask your surgeon, who can explain what they do and do not mean for you.

2. How your plan is made

Your case is carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan. This is then explained to you in person. You are an important part of the team and your wishes matter in every decision.

Most people having treatment with the aim of cure stay under close follow-up for at least five years. If a cancer comes back, it usually does so in the first few years, but not always.

3. When a cancer cannot be cured

We are sorry if you are reading this section because you or someone you love has been given this news. It is a lot to take in, and it is natural to feel shocked, frightened, angry, sad or numb. There is no right or wrong way to feel.

Some words you may hear:

  • Incurable: the cancer has grown too far for treatment to remove it completely, or it has come back after treatment.
  • Metastatic, advanced or stage 4: the cancer has spread to other parts of the body. This may appear months or years after the first treatment, or it may be found at the beginning.
  • Controlled but not cured: treatment may hold the cancer back and keep symptoms in check. Some people live well like this for months or even years.

This does not mean that nothing can be done. Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy or surgery may still be offered, to shrink the cancer, slow it, or relieve symptoms such as pain or difficulty swallowing. Your team will explain what is possible and what each treatment is likely to involve, so that you can decide what matters most to you.

4. Palliative and supportive care

Palliative care is specialist care that aims to improve quality of life for people with an illness that cannot be cured, and for their families. It is not only for people who are dying. It can start early, and can be given at the same time as treatment aimed at the cancer.

  • help with pain, sickness, loss of appetite, breathing, swallowing and other symptoms;
  • emotional, social and spiritual support for you and your family;
  • help with planning ahead and talking with family and friends;
  • care at home, in clinic or in hospital, depending on what is needed and what you would prefer.

The team may include doctors and nurses, a dietitian, a speech and language therapist, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, psychologist, counsellor, social worker, chaplain or spiritual adviser, together with your own GP and the OMFS team. Your family and carers are included in the team too.

Not everyone has pain, but many people do at times, and there are many ways to control it. Please tell your team if you are in pain or uncomfortable, and do not wait to be asked.

5. Questions you may want to ask

It can help to write questions down and to bring someone with you to take notes. Here are some ideas.

About the cancer

  • What type of cancer do I have and where is it? What is its stage?
  • Has my case been discussed at the MDT, and what did the team recommend?

About treatment and care

  • What are my options, what do you recommend, and why?
  • What would happen if I chose not to have treatment?
  • What side effects might I have now and in the long term, and how can they be managed?
  • How will treatment affect my eating, drinking and speech?
  • Who is in my team and who do I contact if I have a problem?
  • Can I be offered palliative or supportive care alongside my treatment?
  • Am I suitable for a clinical trial?

About what to expect

  • What follow-up tests will I need and how often?
  • What might happen as time goes on, and what should I watch for?
  • Where can my family and I find emotional support?
  • Would you mind if I asked for a second opinion?

6. Looking after yourself and those close to you

  • Allow yourself time. You do not need to make every decision straight away.
  • Talk with people you trust, or ask your team about counselling or a psychologist.
  • Peer support groups, in person or online, can put you in touch with others in a similar position.
  • A social worker can help with practical and financial worries.
  • Your palliative care team can help you decide how and when to tell family and friends.
  • If you feel hopeless, or very low, please tell us. Help is available.
You are not alone. Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team will continue to care for you, whatever the plan.

Questions patients often ask

Will you tell me how long I have?

Only if you want to know. Some people want to hear everything and others prefer not to. Doctors cannot predict exactly, but your team can talk with you honestly and kindly, at the pace you choose.

Does palliative care mean treatment has stopped?

No. It can be given alongside treatment aimed at the cancer. Its purpose is comfort, support and quality of life.

Can I get a second opinion?

Yes, and your team will not mind. Ask us and we will help arrange the information needed.

Can I bring someone to my appointments?

Yes, please do. A friend or family member can listen, take notes and help you remember what was said.
