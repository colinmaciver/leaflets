Radiotherapy for Head and Neck Cancer

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains what radiotherapy involves and what to expect during and after it. It is general information to help you talk with your surgeon and OMFS clinic team, and with your radiotherapy (oncology) team.

At a glance
  • Radiotherapy uses carefully aimed high-energy X-rays to destroy cancer cells.
  • It is usually given as a short daily session, Monday to Friday, over several weeks (up to about 7 weeks).
  • You wear a custom-made plastic mask so that you are in exactly the same position each time. The treatment itself is painless.
  • Side effects such as a sore mouth, dry mouth, tiredness and skin changes are common. They are usually worst near the end and for a couple of weeks afterwards, then improve.
  • A dental check before you start is an important part of your preparation.

1. What is radiotherapy and why is it used?

Radiotherapy uses high-energy X-rays to damage cancer cells so that they cannot grow. Like surgery, it is a local treatment, so it mainly affects the area it is aimed at. Healthy cells nearby can be affected too, but modern techniques shape the beams carefully to protect surrounding structures as far as possible.

The total dose is split into small daily treatments (called fractions). This gives healthy cells time to repair between sessions, while cancer cells are less able to repair themselves.

Why might it be recommended?

  • To cure the cancer, sometimes on its own and sometimes together with chemotherapy.
  • After surgery, to destroy any cancer cells that may have been left behind.
  • Occasionally before surgery, to shrink a cancer so that it can be removed.
  • To ease symptoms such as pain or bleeding if a cure is not possible.

Your case will be carefully discussed at the head and neck multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where specialists from different fields agree on the most appropriate plan, and this will then be explained to you in person before any treatment goes ahead.

Radiotherapy is nearly always external beam radiotherapy, where a machine called a linear accelerator directs the beams at the cancer from outside the body. Placing radioactive sources inside or next to the cancer (brachytherapy) is uncommon and only used in special situations.

2. Getting ready: planning and the mask

You will first meet a radiotherapy specialist (clinical oncologist), who will examine you, explain the treatment and arrange any extra scans or blood tests. A separate planning appointment follows.

  • The mask. A mask is moulded to fit your face, head and shoulders. It is made of a mesh-like plastic with air holes, so you can see and breathe through it. You wear it for every treatment so that you are held in exactly the same position.
  • The planning scan. A CT scan is taken while you wear the mask. Your earlier MRI or PET-CT scans may also be used to plan the beams.
  • Your team. Radiographers (radiotherapy therapists), nurses, dietitians, speech and swallowing therapists, and your OMFS team all work together to support you.

If wearing the mask worries you, please say so. Many people find it uncomfortable at first, and your radiographers can help with ways to make it easier.

Look after your mouth first

A dental check before radiotherapy starts lowers the risk of problems later. Teeth that are badly decayed, infected or loose may need to be taken out beforehand, because the jaw may not heal well after radiation. Please see our leaflet on teeth and mouth care for more detail.

3. What happens at each treatment

  • You lie on the treatment couch in the same position as at planning, wearing your mask.
  • Lasers and lights help the radiographers check your position, and a quick scan or X-ray may be taken first.
  • The radiographers then leave the room, but they watch you on camera and can hear and talk to you. You can signal if you need help.
  • The machine moves around you and does not touch you. You cannot see, hear or feel the radiation.

Each visit usually takes around 15 to 30 minutes, most of which is getting you into position. You are not radioactive afterwards and it is safe to be around other people, including children.

You will usually see your oncology team and nurse about once a week during treatment. You can ask for help at any time and do not need to wait for your next review.

4. Side effects and how they are managed

Everyone's experience is different, and you will not necessarily have all of these. They depend on the area treated, the length of treatment and whether you are also having chemotherapy. Side effects often build up during treatment, are usually at their worst in the last two weeks and the first couple of weeks afterwards, and then gradually settle over the following weeks.

Common during treatment

  • Tiredness. Rest when you need to and keep up gentle activity such as short walks.
  • Sore mouth and throat (mucositis). The lining of the mouth and throat becomes inflamed and may ulcerate. Pain relief and special mouthwashes are available.
  • Dry mouth and thick saliva, and changes in taste or loss of appetite.
  • Difficulty swallowing and weight loss. Your dietitian and speech and swallowing therapist will help. Some people need a feeding tube for a time.
  • Skin changes in the treated area, like sunburn: redness, dryness, itching and sometimes soreness or peeling. Your radiotherapy team will give you skin-care advice. Please follow it rather than using creams of your own.
  • Voice or swallowing changes, depending on the area treated.

Late effects

Some effects can appear months or years later. They may include long-term dry mouth, tooth decay, stiffness of the jaw or neck, swelling of the neck or face, and swallowing changes. Rarely, the jawbone can be damaged (osteoradionecrosis). Your oncologist will explain your own risks and how they can be managed.

Please tell your team early about any new or worsening symptom. Problems are much easier to manage when they are caught early.

5. After treatment and the expected outcome

After radiotherapy ends, you will be seen frequently at first to check your recovery and how the cancer has responded. Scans or other tests may be arranged. If all is well, visits become less frequent after the first two to three years. Your own GP will also be part of your long-term care, so please keep your team updated about who this is.

Many side effects improve in the weeks and months after treatment, although some, such as dry mouth, can be long-lasting. Stopping smoking and avoiding alcohol during and after treatment helps healing and improves the chance of cure.

Questions you may wish to ask

  • Is the aim of radiotherapy to cure the cancer or to control it?
  • How many sessions will I have?
  • Will I need chemotherapy or further treatment as well?
  • Which side effects should I expect, and who do I contact if they worry me?
  • What long-term effects are possible, and what follow-up will I need?

Questions patients often ask

Will radiotherapy hurt?

The treatment itself is painless. The side effects, such as a sore mouth or throat, can be uncomfortable, but they can be treated.

Can I keep working?

Many people manage part of their normal routine, especially early in treatment, but tiredness builds up. Talk to your team about what suits you.

What if I feel claustrophobic in the mask?

Tell your radiographers early. They can talk you through it, try different approaches and take things step by step.

Am I radioactive?

No. External beam radiotherapy does not make you radioactive, and you can safely be close to family and friends.

Do I need to miss treatment days if I feel unwell?

Gaps can reduce how well radiotherapy works, so please contact your team before skipping a session. They would rather help you through it.
