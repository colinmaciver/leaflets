Dry Mouth (Xerostomia)

Patient information leaflet

This leaflet explains why your mouth may feel dry, how it is assessed, and what you can do about it. Your surgeon and OMFS clinic team can help if simple measures are not enough.

At a glance
  • Dry mouth happens when you do not make enough saliva, or when the saliva you make is thicker than normal.
  • Common causes are medicines, radiotherapy to the head and neck, diabetes, and conditions such as Sjogren's syndrome.
  • Saliva protects your teeth and gums, so a dry mouth raises the risk of tooth decay, gum disease and infections.
  • Sipping water, saliva substitutes, sugar-free gum and good mouth care help.
  • See a dentist regularly, and have the cause checked.

1. What is dry mouth and why does it happen?

Dry mouth (xerostomia) is the feeling that your mouth is dry. It is often caused by too little saliva (spit), which is made by glands in your cheeks, under the tongue and under the jaw. Saliva keeps the mouth moist, helps you chew, swallow and taste, fights germs and protects your teeth. Dry mouth is common, especially in older people.

Common symptoms

  • A sticky, dry feeling in the mouth and throat
  • Difficulty chewing, swallowing dry food, speaking or tasting
  • A sore, burning or cracked tongue and lips
  • Bad breath, and a need to sip water often, especially at night
  • Dentures that feel loose or sore
  • Frequent tooth decay, gum problems or mouth infections such as thrush

Common causes

  • Medicines. This is the most common cause. Hundreds of medicines can dry the mouth, including some for depression, anxiety, allergy, blood pressure, bladder problems and pain.
  • Radiotherapy to the head and neck. This can damage the salivary glands. It may be long-lasting.
  • Dehydration, mouth breathing or snoring.
  • Diabetes, particularly when sugar levels are high.
  • Sjogren's syndrome. This is an immune condition that attacks the glands that make saliva and tears. It often causes dry eyes too.
  • Smoking, alcohol, caffeine and some mouthwashes containing alcohol.
  • Other causes include salivary gland stones or infection, some surgery, and anxiety.

Dry mouth is a symptom and not a disease in itself, so it is worth finding the cause.

2. How is it assessed?

Your surgeon will ask about your symptoms, your medicines, past treatment such as radiotherapy, and any dry eyes or joint pains. They will examine your mouth, teeth, tongue and the salivary glands, and feel the glands for lumps or swelling.

Tests are not always needed. Depending on the findings, they may include blood tests (for diabetes or Sjogren's syndrome), measuring how much saliva you make, an ultrasound scan of the salivary glands, or occasionally a small biopsy of a minor salivary gland from the inside of the lip. If a medicine is the likely cause, your doctor may be able to change the dose or switch to another. Please do not stop any medicine without advice.

3. What can I do to help myself?

Easing the dryness

  • Sip water often during the day and keep a glass by your bed.
  • Chew sugar-free gum or suck sugar-free sweets or lozenges. These help your own glands make more saliva.
  • Use a saliva substitute (spray, gel or lozenge). Your GP, dentist or pharmacist can advise. Some are available on prescription.
  • Use a lip balm for dry lips.
  • Try a humidifier in the bedroom.
  • Breathe through your nose if you can.

Things that make it worse

  • Smoking and alcohol
  • Too much tea, coffee and fizzy drinks
  • Alcohol-based mouthwashes
  • Very salty, spicy, dry or sugary foods

Protecting your teeth

  • Brush twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste. Your dentist may advise a higher-strength fluoride toothpaste.
  • Clean between your teeth daily.
  • Avoid sugary snacks and drinks. If you do have them, have them with meals only.
  • See your dentist at least every 3 to 6 months. Fluoride varnish may be advised.
  • If you wear dentures, take them out at night and clean them well.

Eating

Moist, soft foods with sauces or gravy are easier. Take sips of water with meals. Avoid very dry or crumbly food.

4. What if self-care is not enough?

  • Saliva-stimulating medicines. If your glands still work, tablets such as pilocarpine can help some people make more saliva. They may cause sweating, flushing and a need to pass water more often, and are not suitable for everyone.
  • Treating the cause. Better control of diabetes, a change of medicine, or treatment of Sjogren's syndrome by a specialist.
  • Treating complications. Thrush needs antifungal treatment. Salivary gland stones or infections may need further assessment or a small procedure.
  • After radiotherapy. Your team will talk to you about options and support. Dryness may improve slowly over months to a year or two, but may not go away completely.

Some of the medicines used for dry mouth are only suitable for certain people and will be prescribed by your doctor or specialist.

5. What is the outlook?

It depends on the cause. If a medicine is the cause, symptoms often improve once it is changed. If the glands are permanently damaged, such as after radiotherapy or with Sjogren's syndrome, dry mouth may be long-term. Even then, regular care keeps most people comfortable and keeps their teeth healthy.

Please see your doctor or dentist if you have a sore patch or ulcer in the mouth that lasts more than 3 weeks, swelling in the cheek or under the jaw, trouble swallowing, or repeated mouth infections.

Questions patients often ask

Is dry mouth dangerous?

Not in itself, but without enough saliva your teeth are at much higher risk of decay and your mouth is more prone to infection. Good mouth care really matters.

Is dry mouth a normal part of ageing?

Ageing alone does not usually cause dry mouth. It is mostly caused by medicines or health conditions, which are more common as we get older.

Does drinking more water cure it?

Sipping water helps comfort but does not fix the underlying problem. Saliva substitutes and sugar-free gum often help more.

Can I stop my tablets if I think they dry my mouth?

Please do not stop them yourself. Speak to your doctor or pharmacist, who may be able to adjust them.
